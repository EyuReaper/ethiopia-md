# Ethiopian Airlines: Africa’s Largest Carrier and the Addis Ababa Hub

Ethiopian Airlines (የኢትዮጵያ አየር መንገድ) was founded in December 1945 by the Ethiopian government in partnership with the American carrier Trans World Airlines (TWA), starting with five war-surplus Douglas C-47 aircraft ([SOAS: Flying High, a case study of Ethiopian Airlines](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/SOAS%20DLD%20Case%20Study%20Ethiopian%20Airlines.pdf)). Eighty years later it is the largest airline group in Africa. In the fiscal year ending July 7, 2026, it reported revenue of 9.1 billion US dollars, up 20 percent, and carried 20.7 million passengers ([Reuters via TimesLIVE: Ethiopian Airlines posts strong growth in revenue](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/africa/2026-07-29-ethiopian-airlines-posts-strong-growth-in-revenue/)). It is state-owned, and the fact that a state enterprise in a low-income country has stayed profitable for decades is why SOAS and others study it, though the record also includes a fatal 2019 crash and the strain of regional conflicts.

## Founding and "Ethiopianization"

Emperor Haile Selassie backed a national airline in 1945, when, according to SOAS, Ethiopia lacked technicians and even a runway. He saw air transport as a way to bind together a country with difficult terrain and few roads or railways ([SOAS](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/SOAS%20DLD%20Case%20Study%20Ethiopian%20Airlines.pdf)). The first scheduled flight, in April 1946, went from Addis Ababa to Cairo via Asmara ([Xinhua via The Star: Ethiopian Airlines celebrates 80 years of operation](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/world/2026/05/03/ethiopian-airlines-celebrates-80-years-of-operation-reaching-145-global-destinations)).

Under management contracts, TWA selected and bought aircraft, supplied most staff and set standards. The 1953 agreement stated that "the ultimate aim is that Ethiopian Air Lines shall eventually be operated by Ethiopian personnel." That was a hard goal in a country with an illiteracy rate of 96 percent and no university. The airline's answer was its own training: the Ethiopian Aviation Academy opened in 1956, an aviation maintenance technician school in 1962 and what SOAS calls Africa's first pilot school in 1964. Semret Medhane became the first Ethiopian chief executive in 1971, and by its 25th anniversary the airline was run entirely by Ethiopians. TWA's role shrank to advice under a fifth agreement in 1970, and the partnership ended in 1975 ([SOAS](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/SOAS%20DLD%20Case%20Study%20Ethiopian%20Airlines.pdf)).

## Surviving Regime Changes

The airline outlasted the empire. After the Derg took power in 1974 it appointed a military general as chief executive, but by 1980, under pressure from management, the government replaced him with an aviation professional who accepted only on condition that state interference stop. In 1991, as EPRDF forces approached Addis Ababa, managers moved aircraft and operations to Nairobi to protect them. Long-serving chief executive Tewolde GebreMariam, in post until 2023, oversaw a fourfold growth in revenue and fleet under the "Vision 2025" plan between 2011 and 2022, and the airline joined Star Alliance in 2011 ([SOAS](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/SOAS%20DLD%20Case%20Study%20Ethiopian%20Airlines.pdf)).

## The Network Today

Reported fleet and network sizes vary with the date and with whether partner airlines are counted. At the airline's 80th-anniversary event in May 2026, Group CEO Mesfin Tasew said it operated 147 aircraft and served 145 international destinations across five continents ([Xinhua via The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/world/2026/05/03/ethiopian-airlines-celebrates-80-years-of-operation-reaching-145-global-destinations)). In July 2026 Reuters put the fleet at more than 150 aircraft, with nine added during the year ([Reuters via TimesLIVE](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/africa/2026-07-29-ethiopian-airlines-posts-strong-growth-in-revenue/)), while in March 2026 Mesfin gave a figure of about 170 including aircraft used by partner airlines ([Capital: Ethiopian Airlines steadies amid Gulf crisis](https://capitalethiopia.com/2026/03/29/ethiopian-airlines-steadies-amid-gulf-crisis/)).

Those partners extend the hub beyond Addis Ababa. Ethiopian has invested in ASKY Airlines in Togo and bought 49 percent of Malawi Airlines, giving it bases in West and Southern Africa ([SOAS](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/SOAS%20DLD%20Case%20Study%20Ethiopian%20Airlines.pdf)).

Growth has been steady but not unbroken. In 2024/25 revenue was 7.6 billion US dollars, up 8 percent; the airline carried over 19 million passengers, 15.2 million of them international, moved 785,323 tonnes of cargo and opened six new international destinations ([2merkato: Ethiopian Airlines reports USD 7.6 billion in revenue](https://www.2merkato.com/news/transport/8571-ethiopian-airlines-reports-usd-76-billion-in-revenue-for-2024/25-fiscal-year)). In 2025/26 cargo rose 16 percent to 897,000 tonnes. The airline did not disclose profit, and the state news agency said cancellations caused by conflict in the Middle East and an Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo had affected results ([Reuters via TimesLIVE](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/africa/2026-07-29-ethiopian-airlines-posts-strong-growth-in-revenue/)).

## Cargo and the Pandemic

Cargo has been a deliberate strategy. The airline first carried Ethiopia's cut flowers on leased freighters, then bought its own freighter fleet. SOAS reports that its Addis Ababa cargo terminal, Africa's largest, can handle one million tonnes a year, and that floriculture now employs about 20,000 people directly and indirectly ([SOAS](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/SOAS%20DLD%20Case%20Study%20Ethiopian%20Airlines.pdf)).

That capability mattered during COVID-19. While many airlines needed government bailouts, Ethiopian converted 25 passenger aircraft to carry cargo, supporting horticulture exporters and delivering millions of vaccine doses across Africa and South America. SOAS describes it as one of only three airlines in the world to stay profitable through the pandemic, posting profits in 2020, 2021 and 2022 with no job losses or pay cuts ([SOAS](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/SOAS%20DLD%20Case%20Study%20Ethiopian%20Airlines.pdf)).

## The 2019 Crash of Flight 302

On March 10, 2019, Ethiopian Airlines flight ET302, a Boeing 737 MAX 8 bound for Nairobi, crashed about six minutes after takeoff from Addis Ababa, killing all 157 people on board, from 35 countries ([Africanews: Ethiopia state investigators release final report on deadly 2019 Boeing 737-8 Max crash](https://www.africanews.com/2022/12/24/ethiopia-state-investigators-releases-final-report-on-deadly-2019-boeing-737-8-max-crash/)). Ethiopia's investigators reported in December 2022 that a faulty angle-of-attack sensor triggered the MCAS flight-control system, which repeatedly pushed the nose down, and faulted Boeing's disclosure of MCAS problems. The US National Transportation Safety Board agreed that MCAS played a role but said the report gave insufficient attention to human performance, attributed the sensor failure most likely to a bird strike rather than an electrical fault, and called a finding on missing crew documentation misleading ([NTSB: Additional Comments on Ethiopia's Final Report on 737 MAX 8 Accident](https://www.ntsb.gov/news/press-releases/Pages/NR20230124.aspx)). The two accounts remain in dispute.

## Vision 2035 and the Bishoftu Airport

The airline's current strategic plan, Vision 2035, was presented at the end of 2022. Africanews reported its targets as growing the fleet from about 140 to 271 aircraft, international destinations from 130 to 207 and annual passengers to more than 60 million ([Africanews: Ethiopian Airlines CEO discusses post-pandemic competitiveness](https://www.africanews.com/2023/07/28/ethiopia-airlines-ceo-discusses-post-pandemic-competitiveness-in-a-changing-industry/)). In March 2026 Mesfin said shortages of wide-body aircraft meant the airline "may not grow as much as outlined" in the plan, with large deliveries not expected until 2029 ([Capital](https://capitalethiopia.com/2026/03/29/ethiopian-airlines-steadies-amid-gulf-crisis/)).

To grow beyond Bole International Airport, the airline is building a new hub. Construction of Bishoftu International Airport, near the town of Bishoftu in Oromia, began on January 10, 2026. The plan has four runways, a first phase for 60 million passengers a year due around 2030 and a final capacity of 110 million. The estimated cost rose from 10 billion US dollars in August 2025 to 12.5 billion. Ethiopian Airlines is to fund 30 percent, the African Development Bank has committed 500 million US dollars, and lenders in the Middle East, Europe, China and the US are expected to cover the rest ([Nairametrics: Ethiopian Airlines begins construction of 110 million passenger Bishoftu Airport](https://nairametrics.com/2026/01/11/ethiopian-airlines-begins-construction-of-110-million-passenger-bishoftu-airport/)).

Ethiopian Airlines shares the national stage with another large state project, the [Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam](/en/economy/grand-ethiopian-renaissance-dam). The [Economy Hub](/en/economy) gives the wider context.
