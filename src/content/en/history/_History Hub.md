# History of Ethiopia

Ethiopia is one of the oldest countries in the world, and beyond being a center for the study of the origins of humankind, it is a rare nation with a history of organized government going back to before the birth of Christ. The fossil of Australopithecus afarensis known as "Lucy," discovered around the month of Hedar in 1967 E.C., lived approximately 3.2 million years ago, and is one of the discoveries that has earned Ethiopia the title "cradle of humankind." From this ancient foundation to the present day, Ethiopia has been a country that has governed itself in the Horn of Africa, and its history encompasses chapters of civilization-building, struggles for independence, and political transition. This overview briefly traces that long journey — from the ancient era of D'mt and Aksum, through the independence victory recorded at Adwa, to the recent building of the federal system.

## Ancient Civilization: From Aksum to the Solomonic Dynasty

Before the Kingdom of Aksum, around the 8th and 5th centuries BC, an ancient kingdom called D'mt (Di'amat) reigned in the area of today's Tigray region; it had close trade and cultural ties with the Sabaean civilization (in today's Yemen) across the Red Sea. The massive temple found at Yeha, built around 700 BC, is one of the oldest standing structures Ethiopia has from this era, and is thought to have most likely been the site of D'mt's capital. The Kingdom of Aksum, which lasted from the first century to the 7th century, was known as a major trading power around the Red Sea; it minted its own currency (coins), built massive monuments (stelae), and was considered one of the leading powers of its era alongside Rome, Persia, and China. King Ezana, around the 4th century, adopted Christianity as the state religion, making Ethiopia one of the first countries in the world to embrace Christianity. [Wikipedia: History of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ethiopia)

After the fall of Aksum, the Zagwe dynasty (roughly from the 890s to around 1262 E.C.) rose to power; the best-remembered legacy of this era is the churches of Lalibela, carved out of solid rock on the orders of King Gebre Meskel Lalibela. Around 1262 E.C., Yekuno Amlak overthrew the last Zagwe king and founded the Solomonic dynasty, which claimed descent from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba; this dynasty, with various interruptions, ruled Ethiopia until 1967 E.C.

## The Victory of Adwa and Resistance to Colonialism

In 1881 E.C., Italy signed the Treaty of Wuchale with Menelik II; however, Article 17 of the treaty differed between the Amharic and Italian versions — the Italian version obligated Ethiopia to conduct all of its foreign relations through Italy, while the Amharic version presented this only as an option. This discrepancy led to war. On Yekatit 23, 1888 E.C., at Adwa, Menelik II fielded between 70,000 and 100,000 fighters and decisively defeated the Italian army led by General Oreste Baratieri. [Britannica: Battle of Adwa](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Adwa) This victory made Ethiopia the first African country to defeat a European power during the colonial era, forcing Italy to recognize Ethiopia's sovereignty.

Yet this history of independence did not pass without challenges. In Meskerem 1928 E.C., fascist Italy, on the orders of Benito Mussolini, invaded Ethiopia; after fierce resistance, it took control of Addis Ababa in Miazia 1928 E.C., and Emperor Haile Selassie went into exile in England. Ethiopians, however, waged a five-year resistance struggle before liberating Addis Ababa on Megabit 28, 1933 E.C., in cooperation with British forces — making Ethiopia the first country to be freed from the control of Axis (Nazi-bloc) forces.

## From Imperial Rule to the Derg Military Regime

Haile Selassie ruled Ethiopia until 1967 E.C., and in 1955 E.C. he played a leading role in the founding of the Organization of African Unity (today's African Union) in Addis Ababa. However, due to grievances arising from economic hardship and famine, a military revolution took place in 1967 E.C. and the emperor was removed from power; power was taken over by a military committee called the "Derg," later led by Colonel Mengistu Haile Mariam. The Derg declared Ethiopia a socialist state, and the "Red Terror" campaign it waged from 1969 E.C. onward to eliminate political opponents took many lives — though the exact death toll varies considerably between sources. [Wikipedia: Red Terror (Ethiopia)](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Terror_(Ethiopia)>) The famine caused by the drought that struck northern Ethiopia (especially Tigray and Wollo) in 1976-1978 E.C. was made worse by the government's concealment of information and its forced-resettlement policy, and became major news around the world. The Derg's rule ended when, after surrendering in the long civil war it had fought against the TPLF and other liberation fronts, Mengistu Haile Mariam fled into exile in Zimbabwe in 1983 E.C.

## The Federal System and Recent History

After the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF), led by Meles Zenawi, took power in 1983 E.C., a new constitution establishing an ethnic-based federal system was ratified in 1987 E.C. Around the same time, when Eritrea seceded from Ethiopia and declared its independence in 1985 E.C., Ethiopia became a country without access to the Red Sea. Meles Zenawi led the country until his death in 2004 E.C. Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, who took power in 2010 E.C., was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2012 E.C. for ending the long-standing conflict with Eritrea. [NPR: How did Ethiopia go from Nobel Peace Prize to war](https://www.npr.org/2023/08/29/1196282658/how-did-ethiopia-go-from-its-leader-winning-the-nobel-peace-prize-to-war-in-a-ye) However, the Tigray War, which broke out between the federal government and the TPLF in Tikimt 2013 E.C., lasted about two years and ended with the cessation-of-hostilities agreement signed in Pretoria in Tikimt 2015 E.C. Ethiopia today continues to face the challenge of reconciling its ethnic federalist system with national unity and stability.

This political transition did not pass without upheaval. In the conflict that erupted following the national election held in Ginbot 1997 E.C., when opposition parties protested that the election results had been rigged, action taken by security forces cost many people their lives, and tens of thousands were also imprisoned. [Wikipedia: 2005 Ethiopian general election violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Ethiopian_general_election_violence) Years later, shortly after Abiy Ahmed came to power, the parties that made up the EPRDF (including the Amhara, Oromo, and Southern Ethiopian Peoples' Democratic parties) merged in Hedar 2012 E.C. to form a single national party called the Prosperity Party; the TPLF, however, did not join the merger, and this move is said to be linked to the political division that, a short time later, led to the Tigray War. [Wikipedia: Prosperity Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosperity_Party)
