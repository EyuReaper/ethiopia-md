# Bale Mountains National Park: Africa’s Largest Afroalpine Plateau and Water Tower

Bale Mountains National Park (የባሌ ተራሮች ብሔራዊ ፓርክ) covers 215,000 hectares of the Bale-Arsi massif in the southeastern Ethiopian highlands, in the Oromia Region. It contains Africa's largest area of afroalpine habitat above 3,000 meters, including the Sanetti Plateau and Tullu Dimtu, at 4,377 meters Ethiopia's second-highest peak. [UNESCO: Bale Mountains National Park](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) The park was created in 1970 and inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023. [Ethiopian Wolf Conservation Programme: Afroalpine Ecosystem](https://www.ethiopianwolf.org/the-wolves/afroalpine-ecosystem) [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) It holds the most important remaining population of the Ethiopian wolf, about two-thirds of the world's mountain nyala, and the source waters of rivers that an estimated 12 million people depend on. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) [Farm Africa / BERSMP: The Significance of the Bale Mountains (2007)](https://farmafrica.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Policy-Brief-1-BERSMP.pdf)

## A Landscape Built by Lava and Ice

UNESCO describes Bale as a landscape shaped by ancient lava flows, by glaciation and by the cutting of the Great Rift Valley. It has volcanic peaks and ridges, escarpments, glacial lakes, gorges and waterfalls. The park spans almost 2,900 meters of elevation, from Tullu Dimtu down to about 1,500 meters in the Harenna Forest. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) Unusual grooves in boulders on some of the shallow slopes remain, in UNESCO's words, "an enigma to geologists and glaciologists". [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/)

## Three Main Zones

**Grasslands and montane forests.** Below the plateau, extensive grasslands lie next to tree-heath, bamboo and juniper forests. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) This is good habitat for the mountain nyala (_Tragelaphus buxtoni_), an endangered antelope found only in Ethiopia. A 2012 habitat model of the Bale Mountains found that only about 17 percent of the study area was good or optimal nyala habitat, much of it on remote southern and southeastern slopes. The rest had been degraded, largely by human use. [Evangelista et al.: Assessing habitat quality of the mountain nyala in the Bale Mountains (Current Zoology, 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1093/czoolo/58.4.525)

**The Sanetti Plateau.** Above 3,500 meters, the Sanetti Plateau and the upper slopes form what UNESCO calls the largest intact and contiguous expanse of afroalpine habitat in the world. More than 80 percent of the species found in this habitat are endemic. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) The giant lobelia (_Lobelia rhynchopetalum_), an Ethiopian endemic that can reach about 6.5 meters with its flower spike, rises above the low vegetation. [Wikipedia: Lobelia rhynchopetalum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobelia_rhynchopetalum) [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) The plateau is also home to the giant mole-rat (_Tachyoryctes macrocephalus_). At the time of inscription it was found only in Bale, and it is the Ethiopian wolf's main prey. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) [Farm Africa / BERSMP](https://farmafrica.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Policy-Brief-1-BERSMP.pdf)

**The Harenna Forest.** South of the plateau, the land drops steeply into the Harenna Forest. Together with the adjoining Mena Angetu forest, it forms the second-largest moist tropical forest in Ethiopia and includes the country's only remaining patches of cloud forest. About 100,000 hectares of the forest are protected within the park and nearby areas. It contains wild forest coffee and medicinal plants, and its bamboo belt is home to the Bale monkey. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/)

## Species Found Nowhere Else

UNESCO's figures for the park are 1,660 documented species of flowering plants, 177 of them endemic to Ethiopia and 31 found only in Bale. It also records 79 mammal species, 23 of them endemic, including eight rodents, and 363 bird species, more than 170 of which are migrants. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) Besides the giant mole-rat, the Harenna shrew, Malcolm's Ethiopian toad, the Bale Mountains tree frog and the Bale Mountains frog were all known only from these mountains when the site was inscribed. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/)

The Ethiopian wolf is the park's best-known animal. Across Ethiopia, the Ethiopian Wolf Conservation Programme estimates that about 500 wolves survive in six small populations, with 453 counted in its latest estimate. [Ethiopian Wolf Conservation Programme](https://www.ethiopianwolf.org/) UNESCO describes Bale's population as the most important of these. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) The programme names habitat loss and diseases carried by domestic dogs as the main threats. [Ethiopian Wolf Conservation Programme](https://www.ethiopianwolf.org/)

## The Water Tower of the Southeast

Heavy orographic rainfall on the massif feeds four major rivers: the Wabe Shebelle, Web, Wemel and Dumal. The same rainfall also supplies numerous springs. A 2007 policy brief from the Bale Eco-Region Sustainable Management Programme estimated that about 12 million people depend on water that originates in the Bale Mountains. [Farm Africa / BERSMP](https://farmafrica.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Policy-Brief-1-BERSMP.pdf) UNESCO states more generally that the park's ecosystems regulate water supply for "millions of people in and beyond Ethiopia". [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/)

## Ice-Age Foragers on the Plateau

Bale also holds the earliest known evidence of people living at high altitude. At the Fincha Habera rock shelter, at about 3,470 meters (11,381 feet), Middle Stone Age foragers lived repeatedly between about 47,000 and 31,000 years ago, while glaciers covered parts of the mountains. [Ossendorf et al.: Middle Stone Age foragers resided in high elevations of the glaciated Bale Mountains, Ethiopia (Science, 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaw8942) [AAAS: Middle Stone Age Human Settlement in Ethiopia Reaches New Heights](https://www.aaas.org/news/middle-stone-age-human-settlement-ethiopia-reaches-new-heights) They made tools from obsidian collected at outcrops about 10 kilometers (6 miles) away and more than 600 meters higher. Large numbers of burnt giant mole-rat bones suggest the rodents were a staple food. A fragment of ostrich eggshell, which must have come from the lowlands, points to contact with other groups. The authors suggest that the highlands may have served as a refuge during the dry glacial period. [AAAS](https://www.aaas.org/news/middle-stone-age-human-settlement-ethiopia-reaches-new-heights)

## Conservation Under Pressure

The park is managed by the Ethiopian Wildlife Conservation Authority (EWCA) and had about 80 rangers when it was inscribed. It is surrounded by a recognized buffer zone of 235,121 hectares that takes in all 29 neighboring _kebeles_, Ethiopia's smallest administrative units. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) UNESCO is frank about the pressures: settlement inside and around the park, the spread of livestock grazing and farming, and a road that crosses key habitats. Management plans aim to reduce livestock gradually and expand no-grazing zones with the consent of affected communities. UNESCO also notes "sporadic civil unrest" as a challenge beyond the park's control. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/111/) Since 2000, new and extended protected areas and community-based conservation have raised the share of suitable Ethiopian wolf habitat under protection from 40 percent to 87 percent (as of 2011). [Ethiopian Wolf Conservation Programme: Afroalpine Ecosystem](https://www.ethiopianwolf.org/the-wolves/afroalpine-ecosystem)

The wild coffee of the Harenna Forest connects Bale to [Ethiopian coffee culture](/en/culture/ethiopian-coffee-culture). For the species that make Ethiopia's highlands distinctive, see [Endemic Wildlife of Ethiopia](/en/nature/ethiopian-endemic-wildlife).
