# The Gadaa System: The Indigenous Democratic Heritage of the Oromo

_Gadaa_ (Afaan Oromoo: Gadaa; Amharic: ገዳ) is the system of generation grades through which Oromo communities have organized political, economic, social and religious life, rotating leadership on a fixed eight-year cycle. In 2016 UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, describing a system in which one of five classes rules at a time, with a chairperson, officials and an assembly, and hands over authority every eight years ([UNESCO: Gada system, an indigenous democratic socio-political system of the Oromo](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/gada-system-an-indigenous-democratic-socio-political-system-of-the-oromo-01164)). The system is still active: in March 2025 the Borana Oromo of southern Ethiopia and northern Kenya installed Guyo Boru Guyo as their 72nd _Abbaa Gadaa_ ([BBC News via The Star: Thousands celebrate a chief who will only rule for eight years](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/africa/2025-03-12-thousands-celebrate-chief-who-will-only-rule-for-8-years)). Scholars and Oromo intellectuals still disagree about how far Gadaa can serve as a model for modern democracy, and that debate is part of its story.

## Generation Classes and the Eight-Year Rhythm

Gadaa is built on _luba_, generation classes. A boy is not placed in a class by his own age but by his father's: a son enters the system a fixed number of grades behind his father, and his whole class then moves together from one grade to the next. UNESCO's summary says membership is open to men whose fathers are already members, that a class "progresses through a series of grades before it can function in authority," and that leadership changes hands every eight years ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/gada-system-an-indigenous-democratic-socio-political-system-of-the-oromo-01164)).

Popular accounts often present a neat ladder of five eight-year stages from birth to age 40. The ethnographic record is more complicated. Among the Borana, Gen Tagawa lists eight named grades (_dabballe_, _gamme_, _kuusa_, _raaba_, _doori_, _gadaa_, _yuuba_ and _gadaamojji_) of unequal length, from five years for _doori_ to 27 for _yuuba_, while a new generation class is still formed every eight years ([Tagawa, Nilo-Ethiopian Studies 22 (2017): The Logic of a Generation-Set System and Age-Set System](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/niloethiopian/2017/22/2017_15/_article/-char/en)). Because a class keeps adding members after it forms, the age spread inside it grows over time. Earlier anthropologists called this a structural flaw; Tagawa argues from Borana testimony that the age gap is necessary to how the system works.

The anchor work on this structure is Asmarom Legesse's _Gada: Three Approaches to the Study of African Society_ (Free Press, 1973), based on his research among the Borana ([Bulletin of SOAS: review of Legesse, Gada (1973)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/asmarom-legesse-gada-three-approaches-to-the-study-of-african-society-xii-340-pp-new-york-free-press-london-colliermacmillan-ltd-c1973-550/BBDEBC20BE6CEB94C04AD4B81DE472EC)).

## The Abbaa Gadaa and the Assembly

When a class reaches the _gadaa_ grade, it takes office for eight years. Its leader is the _Abbaa Gadaa_ ("father of Gadaa"). He governs with other office-holders, not alone: commentary on the Borana system describes councils of _hayyuu_ (counselors), an _Abbaa Seera_ responsible for law, and the _Qaalluu_ as a ritual authority ([African Arguments: What African Elections Can Learn from the Boorana Gadaa System](https://africanarguments.org/?p=48469)). Among the Borana, the leadership rotates among five _gogeessa_ (party lines), so the same line returns to power every 40 years.

The highest body is the general assembly, called _Gumii Gaayoo_ among the Borana, which reviews laws, settles disputes and holds leaders to account ([African Arguments](https://africanarguments.org/?p=48469)). UNESCO notes that meetings and ceremonies are held under a sycamore tree, the _odaa_, which serves as the symbol of Gadaa, and that each major clan has its own Gadaa centers and ceremonial grounds ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/gada-system-an-indigenous-democratic-socio-political-system-of-the-oromo-01164)).

The handover is a public event. The 2025 ceremony lasted a week at Arda Jila Badhasa near Arero. The outgoing leader was 48, and his successor, 37, had been chosen because he impressed the council of elders as a teenager. Men aged 28 to 32, marked by ostrich feathers, already include the next Abbaa Gadaa, due to take office in 2033 ([BBC News via The Star](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/africa/2025-03-12-thousands-celebrate-chief-who-will-only-rule-for-8-years)).

## How Old Is Gadaa?

Gadaa is often described as more than five centuries old. That figure comes from the Borana count of office-holders: 72 Abbaa Gadaa at eight years each comes to roughly 576 years, a calculation made, for example, in African Arguments ([African Arguments](https://africanarguments.org/?p=48469)). It is a genealogical reckoning kept in oral tradition, not a date fixed by written records, and it is best read that way. UNESCO's file describes how oral historians teach each class the system's history, laws, rituals and time-reckoning, and how knowledge is also passed on at home and at school ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/gada-system-an-indigenous-democratic-socio-political-system-of-the-oromo-01164)).

## Women and the Siiqqee Institution

UNESCO's summary is blunt that Gadaa offices are held by men, and that women are consulted on decisions concerning women's rights ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/gada-system-an-indigenous-democratic-socio-political-system-of-the-oromo-01164)). The BBC report on the 2025 handover adds that women cannot hold the top office, sit on the council of elders or be initiated as children ([BBC News via The Star](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/africa/2025-03-12-thousands-celebrate-chief-who-will-only-rule-for-8-years)).

Against that, Oromo scholars point to _siiqqee_ (also written _siinqee_), a ritual stick given to a bride at marriage, paired with the _horooroo_ given to the groom. Drawing on Kuwee Kumsa's 1997 study, anthropologist Marco Bassi summarizes how women use the siiqqee to mobilize collective solidarity, to protest abuses so that they are dealt with under Gadaa law, and to bless, curse or reconcile. Newly elected officers pass under the raised siiqqee of women to receive their blessing. On this reading, siiqqee is a parallel institution that adds another layer of checks and balances, and Asafa Jalata has proposed writing "Gadaa/Siiqqee" to make the pairing explicit ([Bassi, Gadaa Across Domains (Edizioni Ca' Foscari): Introduction to Part 1](https://edizionicafoscari.it/media/pdf/books/978-88-6969-885-9/978-88-6969-885-9-ch-01.pdf)). Bassi also records the opposing criticism, that the system excludes women from political and military office. Both views are part of the current debate.

## Ritual Institution or Constitution?

The deepest scholarly argument about Gadaa is what kind of institution it is. Bassi describes a long dispute between Paul Baxter, who placed Gadaa mainly in the ritual sphere, and Legesse, who answered in _Oromo Democracy: An Indigenous African Political System_ (2000) that Gadaa offers constitutional principles a modern state could draw on. Bassi now calls the ritual-versus-political question "a false problem": ritual and power are linked in every society, and Gadaa is "a highly political institution" in which social and cosmic order are bound together ([Bassi](https://edizionicafoscari.it/media/pdf/books/978-88-6969-885-9/978-88-6969-885-9-ch-01.pdf)). Bassi also notes that the Borana long kept their customary institutions separate from the Ethiopian state, which may have protected them.

Today Gadaa is cited in arguments about term limits and peaceful handovers in African politics ([African Arguments](https://africanarguments.org/?p=48469)), and in Ethiopia it is a strong symbol of Oromo identity. Readers should keep the two levels apart: the documented practice of particular Oromo communities, which varies by region and has changed over time, and Gadaa as a political ideal invoked by later writers.

Gadaa is one of several community institutions that organize Ethiopian life outside the state.
