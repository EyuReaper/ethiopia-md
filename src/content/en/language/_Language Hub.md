# Languages of Ethiopia

## In 30 Seconds

Ethiopia is one of the most linguistically diverse countries in Africa. The language database Ethnologue lists 87 living indigenous languages in the country and 2 that are now extinct; other sources, depending on how they count, put the figure anywhere from 90 to 109. Most of these languages belong to the Semitic, Cushitic and Omotic branches of the Afroasiatic family, with a smaller group belonging to the Nilo-Saharan family. Amharic served as the language of the state for centuries, and the 1995 constitution names it the working language of the federal government. In 2020, the Council of Ministers decided to add Afaan Oromo, Tigrinya, Somali and Afar as further federal working languages. Ethiopia is also one of very few African countries that writes in an indigenous script of its own: the Ge'ez script, or fidel.

## Linguistic Diversity and the Four Language Families

The exact number of Ethiopian languages differs between sources, because deciding whether a way of speaking is a "language" or a "dialect" is itself a matter of scholarly debate. Ethnologue's current country page lists 87 living indigenous languages, while Glottolog counts 109. [Ethnologue: Ethiopia](https://www.ethnologue.com/country/ET/) [Wikipedia: Languages of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ethiopia)

Most of them belong to the Afroasiatic family, which appears in Ethiopia in three main branches:

- **Semitic:** Amharic, Tigrinya, Harari, the Gurage languages, Silt'e and Argobba. Classical Ge'ez also belongs to this branch.
- **Cushitic:** the largest branch by number of speakers, including Afaan Oromo, Somali, Afar, Sidama, Hadiyya and Gedeo.
- **Omotic:** spoken mainly in the southwest, with Wolaytta, Gamo and Kafa among the best known. Whether Omotic belongs to Afroasiatic at all is still debated among linguists.

The fourth family is **Nilo-Saharan**, which covers languages spoken along the western and southwestern borders, such as Nuer, Anuak, Mursi and Suri. Ethnologue assigns 15 of Ethiopia's languages to this family. A handful, such as Ongota, have still not been securely classified into any family.

According to the 2007 national census, the most widely spoken first languages were Afaan Oromo (33.8%), Amharic (29.3%), Somali (6.2%) and Tigrinya (5.9%), followed by Sidama (4.0%) and Wolaytta (2.2%). [Wikipedia: Languages of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ethiopia)

## Amharic: From Court Language to Federal Working Language

Amharic is a Semitic language and the second most widely spoken Semitic language in the world, after Arabic. Around 2020 it had an estimated 33 million-plus first-language speakers and more than 25 million second-language speakers. Amharic is not a direct descendant of Ge'ez; it is grouped instead with Gurage, Argobba and Harari in the South Ethio-Semitic branch. [Wikipedia: Amharic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amharic)

In the medieval period, Amharic became the everyday language of the royal court, the army and trade, earning the name _lisane negus_ ("tongue of the king"), while Ge'ez remained the written language of the Church. Under Emperor Haile Selassie, the revised constitution of 1955 made Amharic the official language of the country. [Al-Ahram Weekly: Amharic, a sovereign tongue in Ethiopia?](https://english.ahram.org.eg/NewsContent/50/0/409637/AlAhram-Weekly/Amharic-A-sovereign-tongue-in-Ethiopia.aspx) Amharic's dominance helped as a shared national medium, but critics argue it also sidelined speakers of other languages, and that argument is still a live political debate.

The 1995 constitution reframed this history in Article 5: "All Ethiopian languages shall enjoy equal state recognition"; "Amharic shall be the working language of the Federal Government"; and members of the Federation (the regional states) "may by law determine their respective working languages." The constitution never uses the term "national language"; Amharic is designated only as a "working language." [Constitute Project: Ethiopia's Constitution of 1994](https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Ethiopia_1994)

### The 2020 Decision: From One to Five

After its meeting on 29 February 2020 (Yekatit 21, 2012 E.C.), the Council of Ministers announced that Afaan Oromo, Tigrinya, Somali and Afar would join Amharic as working languages of the federal government; English was also said to be included under the language use and development policy. [Africanews: One to five, Ethiopia gets four new federal working languages](https://www.africanews.com/2020/03/04/one-to-five-ethiopia-gets-four-new-federal-working-languages/) This was a policy decision, however, and Article 5 of the constitution still names only Amharic. Implementation has been partial: a 2026 analysis called for federal public services and civil-service training in all five languages to be "fully operationalized," which suggests the change exists on paper more than in practice. [Addis Standard via AllAfrica: Economics of Language](https://allafrica.com/stories/202608140051.html)

## The Ge'ez Script and Ge'ez as a Liturgical Language

The Ge'ez script derives from the Ancient South Arabian script and began as an abjad, writing only consonants. During the Aksumite period, in the 4th century, vowels began to be shown by marks added to each consonant; the earliest fully vocalized texts known are the stone inscriptions of King Ezana. Today the script is an abugida in which each consonant has seven "orders" (_ge'ez, ka'ib, salis, rabi', hamis, sadis, sabi'_), one for each vowel. In Amharic and Tigrinya it is usually called _fidel_, and the same script writes Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre, Harari and the Gurage languages. [Wikipedia: Geʽez script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge%CA%BDez_script)

Ge'ez itself stopped being an everyday spoken language sometime between 900 and 1200 CE, but it survives as the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and it is the ancestor of Tigrinya and Tigre. [Britannica: Geʿez language](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Geez-language) Because the liturgy, church chant and parchment manuscripts have been kept in Ge'ez, the language is the archive of Ethiopia's written heritage. For Ge'ez manuscript painting, see [[ethiopian-illuminations-modern-art]].

## Afaan Oromo and the Qubee Alphabet

Afaan Oromo, Ethiopia's largest language by number of speakers, has historically been written in the Ge'ez, Arabic and Latin scripts. The Latin-based Qubee alphabet was chosen in 1972 by a study group of Oromo students in Europe, and in 1991 Oromo scholars meeting in Finfinnee (Addis Ababa) adopted it as the official script of Afaan Oromo. Since 1991, Qubee has been used in schools in Oromia and by Oromo communities in the diaspora. [Curate Oromia: The history and politics of the Qubee alphabet](https://curateoromia.com/the-history-and-politics-of-the-qubee-alphabet/)

Supporters of Qubee argue that Ge'ez does not clearly mark consonant doubling (gemination) or vowel length, both of which matter in Afaan Oromo; opponents point to historical Oromo texts written in fidel. The choice of script is therefore a question of identity and politics as much as a technical one. For the Oromo system of traditional governance, see [[gadaa-system]].

## Language and Federalism

Language is a basic pillar of Ethiopia's federal system. Article 39 of the constitution states that every nation, nationality and people has "the right to speak, to write and to develop its own language," and Article 46 says regional states "shall be delimited on the basis of the settlement patterns, language, identity and consent of the people concerned." [Constitute Project: Ethiopia's Constitution of 1994](https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Ethiopia_1994) On that basis, Oromia uses Afaan Oromo as its regional working language, the Somali Region uses Somali, Afar uses Afar, Tigray uses Tigrinya and Harari uses Harari, while others, including Amhara, Gambela and Benishangul-Gumuz, use Amharic. [Wikipedia: Languages of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ethiopia)

In education, the 1994 Education and Training Policy made primary schooling in nationality languages the norm and moved instruction to English from secondary school onward. Before that, Amharic was the only medium of instruction in formal schooling. [Young Lives Working Paper 108 (Vujcich)](https://www.younglives.org.uk/sites/default/files/migrated/YL-WP108_Vujcich.pdf) According to Ministry of Education data from 2016, 51 languages were being used as media of instruction in different parts of the country. The system has done a great deal to develop these languages, but critics say it also creates language barriers for people moving between regions for work or study.

## Endangered Languages

This diversity is not secure. Gafat, Weyto and Mesmes are already extinct, and Ongota, spoken in southwestern Ethiopia, is classified as Critically Endangered by the UNESCO Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger. A study published in 2000 found that only eight people in a community of nearly one hundred still actively used the language; its speakers have been shifting to the neighboring Ts'amakko language. [Wikipedia: Ongota language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ongota_language) The linguist Hudson, using a threshold of fewer than 10,000 speakers, counted 22 Ethiopian languages as endangered, though critics note that a speaker-count threshold may not fit every case. [Wikipedia: Languages of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ethiopia)
