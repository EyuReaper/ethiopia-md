# Lifestyle 🏠

Ethiopians conduct their daily lives within a framework built on family ties, religious practices, and communal solidarity. From the ancient coffee ceremony to the country's own calendar, from rural agricultural life to rapid urban change, this page offers a brief tour of the facets of Ethiopian lifestyle.

## The Coffee Ceremony: A Center of Social Bonding

In Ethiopia, coffee is a social institution with a meaning that goes beyond being a beverage. According to tradition, coffee was first discovered in the area of Kaffa province in southwestern Ethiopia, where a herder named Kaldi is said to have noticed his goats becoming alert after eating red berries; monks later began using coffee to stay alert for night prayers. ([Britannica: History of Coffee](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-coffee))

The coffee ceremony is traditionally led by women and involves roasting the coffee by hand, grinding it in a mortar, and brewing it in a jebena; incense is also often burned. The ceremony involves serving three rounds of coffee: abol (the first, strong round), tona (the second), and baraka (the light third round, suitable even for children). ([Compassion International](https://www.compassion.com/blog/ethiopian-coffee-ceremony-coffee-and-community/)) Roasted grain (kolo), peanuts, or bread are served alongside the coffee; the ceremony can last two to three hours and becomes an occasion for neighbors and family to talk over the day's affairs, converse, and sometimes even resolve disputes. Being invited to a coffee ceremony is regarded as a sign of friendship and honor.

## Religion and Daily Life

Religion holds a major place in Ethiopians' daily and weekly life. According to recent estimates, followers of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church make up about 43.5% of the country's population, Islam accounts for around 31–33%, and Protestant/Evangelical churches account for around 18.6%. ([Religion in Ethiopia, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Ethiopia)) Orthodox Tewahedo followers are concentrated in the northern part of the country (especially in the Tigray and Amhara regions), while Islam is widespread in the Afar, Oromia, and Somali regions.

The Ethiopian Orthodox Church has an extensive fasting canon: every Wednesday and Friday (except during the Pentecost period) are fasting days. In addition, this includes the Fast of the Prophets (43 days before Christmas), the Great Lent/Hudade fast (55 days before Easter), and the Fast of the Assumption of Mary (15 days in the month of Nehase). This fasting canon shapes not only followers' food habits but also their social life; in total, more than 180 days of the year can be fasting days.

Another distinctive feature tied to religious tradition is the Ethiopian (Ge'ez) calendar. This calendar divides the year into 13 months: 12 months of 30 days each, and a 13th month, Pagume, that has only 5 or 6 days depending on the year. It differs from the Gregorian calendar by about 7–8 years, a difference that arises from the Ethiopian Orthodox Church's calculation relating to the birth of Christ. New Year (Enkutatash) is celebrated on Meskerem 1 (Meskerem 2 in a Matthew leap year / September 11 or 12 in the Gregorian calendar); the name means "precious gifts," and is tied to the tradition linking it to the return of the Queen of Sheba's journey from Jerusalem. ([SBS Amharic](https://www.sbs.com.au/language/amharic/en/article/unravelling-ethiopias-enigmatic-calendar-the-mystery-of-the-seven-year-age-difference/b7pgy3ml6))

This religious diversity is also reflected in the country's calendar of national holidays: Orthodox Tewahedo holidays such as Christmas (Genna), Timkat, and Meskel, as well as Islamic holidays such as Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, are all officially observed national holidays. Timkat was inscribed on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list in Tahsas 2012 E.C., and Meskel in 2006 E.C., which has earned these ceremonies international recognition. ([Ethiopian Embassy](https://ethiopianembassy.org/ethiopian-epiphany-inscribed-on-unesco-intangible-cultural-heritage-lists-december-12-2019/)) During such holidays and wedding ceremonies, many Ethiopian women wear the traditional garment known as the "habesha kemis"; this garment is made from a hand-spun cotton cloth called "shema," and its edges are adorned with traditional embroidery designs called "tilf" or "tilet." ([Wikipedia: Habesha kemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habesha_kemis))

## Family and Community Ties

The Ethiopian family structure is traditionally extended, encompassing multiple generations. When a marriage takes place, it is customary for the wife to move to her husband's family home — a patrilocal settlement custom — so that three generations (parents, sons and their wives, and grandchildren) may live together. ([Cultural Atlas: Ethiopian Culture](https://culturalatlas.sbs.com.au/ethiopian-culture/ethiopian-culture-family)) Beyond parents and siblings, even aunts, uncles, and neighbors take an active part in raising children.

This communal way of life is a bond in which family and neighbors support one another both economically and emotionally. Respect for elders and parents holds a high place in Ethiopian society, and this traditional hierarchy remains especially strong in rural areas. In the cities, however, a trend toward the nuclear (mother-father-children) family structure is emerging, though even when adult children move out of their parents' home, providing economic support often continues.

Family and community ties are also reflected in eating customs. It is common for Ethiopians to eat together from a shared plate (mesob), with stew placed on injera; the custom known as "gursha" refers to the act of one person wrapping a piece of injera and placing it by hand into another's mouth, which is regarded as an expression of love, friendship, and respect. ([LMC Comboni Lay Missionaries](https://lmcomboni.org/blog/en/the-ethiopian-gursha/)) Such small daily customs are counted among the features that express Ethiopians' shared identity and mutual care.

## From Countryside to City: Differences in Lifestyle

Although rapid urbanization is under way, most Ethiopians still live in rural areas; recent estimates show that about 76% of the population lives in rural areas. ([Trading Economics, citing World Bank data](https://tradingeconomics.com/ethiopia/rural-population-percent-of-total-population-wb-data.html)) Rural life is largely based on farming; the average landholding is less than two hectares, and an average of 10 different crop and livestock varieties are produced on a single farm, which plays a crucial role in food security. On average, 58% of rural households' caloric consumption comes from their own production. ([FAO Knowledge Repository: RuLIS Ethiopia](https://openknowledge.fao.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/a97e26a8-6eff-4c5f-a260-b9f6249d18dd/content))

Urban life, however, has a different rhythm: job opportunities, education, and infrastructure services are relatively more accessible. Even so, urban expansion itself brings challenges such as housing shortages, traffic congestion, and unplanned urban settlement. ([UN-Habitat Ethiopia](https://unhabitat.org/ethiopia)) The gap between rural and urban areas — in income, access to education, and way of life — remains one of the major issues shaping Ethiopia's social landscape.
