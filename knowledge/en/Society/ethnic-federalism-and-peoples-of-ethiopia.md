# Ethnic Federalism and the Peoples of Ethiopia

## 30-second overview

Ethiopia is a federation whose regional states are drawn largely along lines of language and identity. Its constitution begins, "We, the Nations, Nationalities and Peoples of Ethiopia" ([Constitution of Ethiopia, via Constitute](https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Ethiopia_1994)). The system is among the most debated features of the country. Supporters see it as recognition of a plural society; critics argue it has made ethnicity the main axis of politics and competition. This article describes what the constitution says, who lives in the country according to the 2007 census, how the map has changed, and what different observers argue. It does not take a side.

## What the Constitution Says

The constitution, ratified in 1994 according to Crisis Group and often dated 1995 in other sources, declares that "all sovereign power resides in the Nations, Nationalities and Peoples of Ethiopia" (Article 8). Article 39 gives every nation, nationality and people "an unconditional right to self-determination, including the right to secession," along with rights to use and develop its language, to express its culture and preserve its history, and to "a full measure of self-government." Secession requires a two-thirds vote of the group's legislative council, a referendum organized by the federal government within three years, and a majority vote in that referendum. Article 39(5) defines a nation, nationality or people as a group sharing "a large measure of a common culture or similar customs, mutual intelligibility of language, belief in a common or related identities," and inhabiting "an identifiable, predominantly contiguous territory." Article 5 gives all Ethiopian languages "equal state recognition," makes Amharic the federal working language, and lets member states choose their own. Article 46 says states are delimited "on the basis of the settlement patterns, language, identity and consent of the people concerned" ([Constitution of Ethiopia](https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Ethiopia_1994)).

Article 47 originally listed nine states: Tigray, Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Benishangul-Gumuz, the Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples, Gambela and Harari. It also lets groups within a state form their own state through a council vote and referendum ([Constitution of Ethiopia](https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Ethiopia_1994)). Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa are federally chartered city administrations.

## The Peoples

The 2007 census, the one most often cited, recorded 95 ethnic groups. A 2022 Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada research response lists those with more than one million people: Oromo (25,363,756), Amhara (19,878,199), Somali (4,586,876), Tigrayan (4,486,513), Sidama (2,951,889), Guragie (1,859,831), Welaita (1,676,128), Afar (1,276,867), Hadiya (1,269,382) and Gamo (1,104,360) ([IRB Canada: Ethiopia: Ethnic groups, via ecoi.net](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2081244.html)). These are 2007 counts, now well over a decade old, and the identity categories themselves are contested; the document describes the system as an "ethnic federation."

## Why It Was Designed This Way

Crisis Group's 2009 report traces the system to the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front, which after removing the Derg in 1991 "radically reformed Ethiopia's political system" and, under the leadership of the Tigray People's Liberation Front, institutionalized "people's rights to self-determination and self-rule." The report says the constitution was "applauded for its commitment to liberal democracy and respect for political freedoms and human rights," but that the regional states are "in fact weak" in practice ([International Crisis Group: Ethiopia: Ethnic Federalism and Its Discontents](https://www.ecoi.net/en/file/local/1107615/1002_1252263010_icg-ethiopia.pdf)). Crisis Group is a conflict-prevention think tank, and the report is a 2009 assessment of the then-ruling coalition, so it describes that period rather than the present.

## What Critics and Supporters Say

Crisis Group's conclusion was that ethnic federalism "has not dampened conflict, but rather increased competition among groups that vie over land and natural resources, as well as administrative boundaries and government budgets," while having "powerfully promoted ethnic self-awareness among all groups." It reports that for Amhara and national elites the system impedes a strong unitary nation-state, while for armed movements such as the Oromo Liberation Front and the Ogaden National Liberation Front it "remains artificial" ([Crisis Group](https://www.ecoi.net/en/file/local/1107615/1002_1252263010_icg-ethiopia.pdf)). Yonatan Fessha of the University of the Western Cape wrote in 2019 that movements representing the Oromo supported the Sidama campaign because they see it as strengthening sub-national citizenship, and that Ethiopia had 14 states before the current constitution reduced them to nine and two self-governing cities ([Fessha: The Sidama have voted for their own state, The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/the-sidama-have-voted-for-their-own-state-what-follows-is-far-less-certain-127748)).

Supporters generally point to the text itself: the rights to language, culture and self-government in Article 39. Critics point to outcomes. The two readings are not strictly opposed; both can be partly true in different regions and periods.

## The Map Keeps Changing

The Sidama referendum on 20 November 2019 produced about 98 percent support for autonomous rule, according to Al Jazeera, which said the Sidama number more than 3 million and that the new state would be Ethiopia's tenth ([Al Jazeera: Ethiopia's Sidama vote for new federal region](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2019/11/23/ethiopias-sidama-vote-for-new-federal-region-electoral-board)). Fessha warned that the vote could open the "floodgates" for other communities and that, with more than 80 ethnic groups, "a territorial solution is not feasible for everyone," adding that micro-units may not be economically viable; he also noted that the Welayta, the second-largest group in the Southern region, had already demanded their own state ([Fessha](https://theconversation.com/the-sidama-have-voted-for-their-own-state-what-follows-is-far-less-certain-127748)). On 5 July 2023 the House of Federation voted unanimously for a 12th regional state, South Ethiopia, made of six zones and five special districts split from the Southern region after a February 2023 referendum, the third new region since 2018 ([ConstitutionNet: In Ethiopia, upper house approves creation of 12th regional state](https://constitutionnet.org/news/ethiopia-upper-house-approves-creation-12th-regional-state)). The nine states in Article 47 therefore no longer describe the country, and we have not checked whether the text has been formally amended.

## Limits of This Article

We rely on the constitution's text, a 2009 think-tank report, a 2022 government research summary, a 2019 academic commentary and two news reports. We did not review Tigrayan, Amhara, Oromo or Somali scholarship in depth, nor the post-2020 conflicts, and statements about contemporary politics should be checked against current reporting.

## Closing

The constitution gave Ethiopia's peoples a formal political voice and a map that has continued to change. A fuller treatment needs primary scholarship from several regions and an up-to-date account of constitutional amendments.
