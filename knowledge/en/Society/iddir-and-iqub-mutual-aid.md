# Iddir and Iqub: Indigenous Social Safety Nets and Rotating Credit

Two member-run institutions carry much of the weight of everyday risk in Ethiopia. The _iddir_ (እድር) is a funeral association: members pay regular dues and, when a death occurs, the group pays out cash and organizes the mourning. The _iqub_ (እቁብ, also written _equb_ or _iqqub_) is a rotating savings and credit association in which members pay a fixed sum into a pool that goes to one member at a time. Neither is a relic. In a survey of 15 Ethiopian villages in the 1990s, about 80 percent of households belonged to at least one iddir, and outside two villages in Tigray where none existed, membership was above 95 percent in almost every village ([Dercon, De Weerdt, Bold and Pankhurst, QEH Working Paper 126: Membership Based Indigenous Insurance Associations in Ethiopia and Tanzania](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps126.pdf)). Both are now moving onto mobile phones.

## Iddir: Insurance Run by Neighbors

An iddir is a group with written or agreed rules about who pays what and what members receive. In the rural Ethiopian groups studied by Stefan Dercon, Joachim De Weerdt, Tessa Bold and Alula Pankhurst, about 80 percent charged a regular contribution, usually monthly; in towns, almost all did. Among 63 groups with regular dues, the average was 1.64 birr a month (median 1 birr, range 0.25 to 5 birr) at the time of the survey, and new members paid an entrance fee of about 42 birr. Groups averaged about 84 members, and households typically belonged to two or three iddirs at once, in one village up to eight ([QEH Working Paper 126](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps126.pdf)).

Funerals are the core, but not the limit. About 64 percent of the groups surveyed gave short-term loans to members, mainly for funerals, illness or a destroyed house, and 64 percent offered some other cover, such as payouts for house destruction, illness, fire, loss of cattle, harvest failure or weddings ([QEH Working Paper 126](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps126.pdf)). The peer-reviewed version of the study concludes that these groups are widespread and inclusive and could help extend insurance and other development work ([Dercon et al., World Development 34(4), 2006: Group-based funeral insurance in Ethiopia and Tanzania](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:wdevel:v:34:y:2006:i:4:p:685-703)).

The same researchers are clear about the limits. Iddirs cover an important part of the cost of some shocks but still leave households exposed to risk. For the poorest quarter of households, monthly iddir payments were about 40 percent of what they spent on health each month ([QEH Working Paper 126](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps126.pdf)).

## Where the Iddir Came From

It is tempting to call the iddir an age-old tradition, and the study's authors warn that this would be "wholly misleading." Mutual help at funerals is old everywhere, but the iddir's form, with written rules, cash dues and records, points to a setting of money, literacy and town life. The available evidence suggests it is an early 20th-century institution, probably started by migrants. The first reported iddir is attributed to Soddo Gurage traders in Addis Ababa at the start of the 20th century. Iddirs multiplied in the capital during the Italian occupation (1936 to 1941) and later spread from towns to the countryside ([QEH Working Paper 126](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps126.pdf)).

Their relationship with the state has shifted. In the 1960s Addis Ababa officials sought to work with iddirs, and some politicians used them as platforms. After the 1974 revolution the Derg treated iddir leaders as reactionary, and most groups withdrew to burial work alone. After 1991 the government and NGOs again looked to iddirs for health and development campaigns ([QEH Working Paper 126](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps126.pdf)). The HIV/AIDS epidemic tested them directly: in a survey of 120 Addis Ababa iddirs, 93 percent reported more deaths among members in the previous three years, about a third had raised contributions or found new income, and about half had started HIV education for members ([QEH Working Paper 126](https://www.qeh.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/pdf_docs/qehwps126.pdf), summarizing Pankhurst and Damen Haile Mariam).

## Iqub: Rotating Savings and Credit

Economists call the iqub a ROSCA. Dejene Aredo defines it as an association in which each member pays a fixed sum into a common pool at regular intervals, and each in turn receives the pool, allocated by drawing lots, by bidding or by other means ([Dejene Aredo, Ethiopian Journal of Development Research 26(1), 2004: The Iqqub](https://ejol.aau.edu.et/index.php/EJDR/article/view/7630)). Members who collect early are in effect borrowing from the rest; those who collect late are saving.

Its strength is flexibility. In the 1950s observers found iqub members ranging from Addis Ababa shoe-shiners paying 10 cents a day to businessmen paying 100 birr a month. In villages Aredo studied, poor women saved one birr a week while shopkeepers saved 30 birr or more, often in separate groups. Payments can be daily, weekly or monthly, a person can hold several shares or split one, and the money can go to anything from school books to business stock ([Aredo](https://ejol.aau.edu.et/index.php/EJDR/article/view/7630)). Aredo links the cash iqub to older in-kind pooling, such as the _wujo_ in Gurage areas, where women pooled milk in turn.

Iqubs are not free of risk. Aredo reports that default is a smaller problem than in formal lending but that urban iqubs often face serious default. Groups protect themselves by screening newcomers (one Addis Ababa businessmen's iqub required a new member to be known by six existing members), by requiring guarantors, and for large pools by asking for title deeds as collateral. Organizers can take defaulters to court on the basis of written by-laws ([Aredo](https://ejol.aau.edu.et/index.php/EJDR/article/view/7630)).

## Saving Outside the Banks

These groups matter because formal finance has reached relatively few Ethiopians. According to the World Bank's 2017 Global Findex survey, 35 percent of Ethiopian adults had an account. Of the 62 percent who saved in the previous year, 26 percent saved at a financial institution and 38 percent saved with someone outside the family or at a savings club, while 8 percent had borrowed from a savings club ([World Bank Blogs: Financial inclusion in Ethiopia, 10 takeaways from the latest Findex](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/africacan/financial-inclusion-in-ethiopia-10-takeaways-from-findex-2017)). Those figures are now dated, and mobile money has grown quickly since, but they show why the iqub and iddir remain central.

## Going Digital

Iqubs are now being built into apps. In March 2021 the startup eQub Financial Technologies launched a platform to digitize the traditional iqub ([Shega: eQub launches a digital saving platform](https://shega.co/news/equb-launches-a-digital-saving-platform)). In August 2024 it signed a partnership with Awash Bank to set up digital iqub groups on the bank's AwashBirr-pro channel, linking the practice to a bank account ([Addis Fortune: Leading Private Bank Joins Force for Digital Equb](https://addisfortune.news/leading-private-bank-joins-force-for-digital-equb)). Neither report gives user numbers, so it is too early to say how far digital iqubs have replaced the paper ledger and the weekly meeting.

Iddir and iqub sit alongside older community institutions such as the Oromo [Gadaa system](/en/society/gadaa-system). The growth of mobile money in Ethiopia is described in [Telebirr and the Digital Payments Revolution](/en/technology/telebirr-digital-payments).
