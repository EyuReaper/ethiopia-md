# Education and Literacy in Ethiopia: Rapid Access, Struggling Quality

## 30-second overview

Since the early 1990s Ethiopia has built one of Africa's fastest expansions of school access. Researchers in the RISE education research programme describe the gross primary enrollment rate as rising to 95 percent by 2013 ([RISE: Ambitious Plans, Poor Outcomes](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2023-06/PEB_Ethiopia.pdf)). Learning has not kept pace: the same brief calls outcomes "dismal." Adult literacy has risen, but it is still low, and the war and drought of recent years have pushed millions of children out of class. This article covers the policy framework, the language-of-instruction debate, literacy trends and the present crisis, with the source of each number.

## Three Regimes, Three Education Systems

The RISE brief, by Asnake Kefale, Belay Hagos Hailu and Alec Gershberg and summarized by Communications Development Incorporated, divides Ethiopia's education politics into the imperial period to 1974, the Derg (1974 to 1991) and the EPRDF era after 1991. It says imperial schooling prepared people for the bureaucracy and "excluded the majority of Ethiopians, particularly in rural areas." The Derg expanded access: primary schools rose from 2,050 in 1974 to 8,260 by 1987, and pupils from under a million in 1975 to more than 2.4 million in 1986. Quality fell as schools opened without trained teachers, and the education budget fell from 17 percent of the national budget in 1974 to 10 percent in the 1980s ([RISE](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2023-06/PEB_Ethiopia.pdf)).

## The 1994 Education and Training Policy

After 1991 the transitional government adopted a new Education and Training Policy in 1994, before the constitution, according to RISE. It decentralized management, promised special help for disadvantaged regions, recognized communities' right to use their languages, allowed private schools, and set a new structure of kindergarten, eight years of primary school in two cycles of four, two years of general secondary and two years of preparatory school ([RISE](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2023-06/PEB_Ethiopia.pdf)). Five Education Sector Development Programs followed, the first launched in 1997. RISE's interviewees said the first three concentrated on access and only the fourth and fifth gave quality more weight. A draft Education Development Roadmap for 2018 to 2030, issued in December 2017, proposed new curricula and textbooks, and RISE says close to 2.5 million people took part in consultations ([RISE](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2023-06/PEB_Ethiopia.pdf)).

## Access Grew Fast

A 2016 Young Lives working paper by Tassew Woldehanna and Aregawi Gebremedhin, using the Ministry of Education's figures, says the primary net enrollment rate almost doubled in the first decade of the century, from 48 percent in 2000/01 to 86 percent in 2012/13 ([Woldehanna and Gebremedhin: Learning Outcomes of Children Aged 12 in Ethiopia](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57c5aa51ed915d6cfa000002/YL-WP152-Learning_outcomes_in_Ethiopia.pdf)). RISE reports government education spending of roughly 25 percent of total spending and the World Bank-supported General Education Quality Improvement Program (2008 to 2012 and 2013 to 2018) ([RISE](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2023-06/PEB_Ethiopia.pdf)).

## Quality Did Not Follow

The Young Lives study compared two groups of 12-year-olds, in 2006 and 2013, and found that reading and mathematics competencies "had fallen substantially." The decline was larger for girls, rural children, children in government schools and those whose caregivers had little education. The authors link this partly to rising dropout (from 12.4 to 16.1 percent) and grade repetition (from 6.1 to 7.9 percent) alongside expanded intake, and they call for research on the causes ([Young Lives](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57c5aa51ed915d6cfa000002/YL-WP152-Learning_outcomes_in_Ethiopia.pdf)). The study is a sample of children in selected sites, not a national census.

RISE adds that a 2010 Early Grade Reading Assessment found a large share of second-graders could not read: the share with zero scores ranged from 69 percent in the Sidama language group to more than 25 percent in the largest regions. Assessments from 2014 to 2018 found about two-thirds of grade 2 and 3 pupils were nonreaders or had limited comprehension. RISE names automatic promotion in grades 1 to 4 as one factor ([RISE](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2023-06/PEB_Ethiopia.pdf)).

## Language of Instruction

Before 1991, according to Alemu and Tekleselassie (2006), Amharic was the medium in elementary school and English from junior high; the 1974-1991 government had encouraged some 15 ethnic languages in non-formal education. The authors say the post-1991 reform changed the media of instruction, with eight local languages used at primary level in the Southern region alone, and they argue that rushing the policy without regard to regional development gaps looked "more of a political gimmick than a sound pedagogical move" ([Alemu and Tekleselassie: Instructional language policy in Ethiopia, Planning and Changing 37](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ756221.pdf)).

A 2025 JSTOR Daily report says schools teach in 51 languages, with mother-tongue instruction in grades 1 to 8 and a move to English in grade 9, though regions may switch earlier, in grade 5 or 7. It cites a 2020 study by Solomon Areaya and Daniel Tefera in which nearly 71 percent of parents wanted the switch to English earlier, while only 9.8 percent wanted English exclusively ([Clawson: Fifty-one languages, but when does English enter the picture?, JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/fifty-one-languages-but-when-does-english-enter-the-picture)). The language policy is thus both a cultural-rights measure and a question about learning outcomes, and the evidence does not yet settle the second.

## Literacy

World Bank World Development Indicators, retrieved through the St. Louis Fed's FRED, give these adult (age 15 and over) literacy rates: 27.0 percent in 1994, 35.9 in 2004, 39.0 in 2007, 48.4 in 2013, 47.5 in 2016, 55.1 in 2019, 54.9 in 2021 and 60.5 in 2022 ([World Bank WDI via FRED: Literacy rate, adult total, Ethiopia](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/SEADTLITRZSETH)). The series is not smooth, with small dips in 2016 and 2021, and the years come from different surveys, so the trend is better read as a rise of about 33 points over 28 years than as a precise annual path.

## Conflict and Closed Schools

UNICEF's humanitarian report for October and November 2024 says, citing the Education Cluster, that over 9 million children were out of school because of conflict, violence, natural disasters and displacement, and that over 10,000 schools, 18 percent of the country's total, had been damaged. It gives the largest shares as Amhara (4.4 million), Oromia (3.2 million) and Tigray (1.2 million) ([UNICEF: Ethiopia Humanitarian Situation Report No. 9](https://www.unicef.org/media/166796/file/UNICEF%20Ethiopia%20Humanitarian%20Sitrep%20No.%209%20October-November%202024.pdf.pdf)). These are humanitarian estimates, and other reports give different totals. We have not found a later figure from a source we could verify.
