# Injera and Teff Agronomy: The Foundation of Ethiopian Cuisine

_Injera_ (እንጀራ) is a sour, fermented, pancake-like flatbread, traditionally made from the flour of _teff_ (ጤፍ, _Eragrostis tef_), a tiny-grained cereal domesticated in the Ethiopian highlands. A 2020 review in the _Journal of Ethnic Foods_ notes that almost all Ethiopians eat injera at least once a day. [Neela & Fanta, Journal of Ethnic Foods (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42779-020-00069-x) Teff, in turn, is Ethiopia's most important staple food crop by both area and value. [Minten et al., IFPRI ESSP Working Paper 52 (2013)](https://hdl.handle.net/10568/153504) At the table, injera is plate, utensil and food at once: stews are ladled on top, diners tear off pieces to scoop them up, and the bread underneath, soaked in sauce, is eaten last. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera)

## Teff: An Ancient Highland Grain

Teff is an annual grass native to the highlands of Ethiopia and Eritrea. Its domestication is usually dated to sometime between 4000 BCE and 1000 BCE, and genetic evidence points to the wild grass _Eragrostis pilosa_ as its most likely ancestor. [Wikipedia: Teff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teff) The seeds are remarkably small, about a millimeter long, and a thousand grains weigh roughly 0.3 grams. Their color ranges from white to deep reddish brown. [Wikipedia: Teff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teff)

Teff has a long history in Ethiopia, but injera as an everyday staple is younger than often assumed. A 2023 study by Hiroki Ishikawa, published by Kyoto University's Center for African Area Studies and based on royal chronicles and European travel accounts, finds that teff consumption spread across northern Ethiopia over about 250 years from the 1520s. Round teff flatbreads had become the region's staple food by the early 1750s. Archaeologists have found teff and clay griddle-like trays at Aksumite sites, but Ishikawa notes that no scholar has shown injera to have been the staple food of the Aksumite kingdom. [Ishikawa, African Study Monographs (2023)](https://hdl.handle.net/2433/282789)

## Nutrition and Grades of Teff

Teff's reputation as a "supergrain" rests on real nutritional qualities. A review for the International Food Policy Research Institute by Kaleab Baye of Addis Ababa University summarizes the evidence. Teff's starch is slowly digestible, and its protein content is similar to wheat's but richer in the essential amino acid lysine. It is also a good source of fiber, essential fatty acids, calcium and iron. [Baye, IFPRI ESSP Working Paper 67 (2014)](https://hdl.handle.net/10568/145205) Teff is gluten-free, and that recognition is what drove much of the global interest in it over the past two decades. [Baye (2014)](https://hdl.handle.net/10568/145205)

Ethiopians grade teff by color: _nech_ (white), _key_ (red) and _sergegna_ (mixed). [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera) Color is also a marker of price and status. Pure teff injera is a symbol of wealth, and white teff costs more than red. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera) IFPRI surveys found that over the decade to 2013, consumers shifted steadily from the cheaper red varieties toward the more expensive white ones. [Minten et al. (2013)](https://hdl.handle.net/10568/153504)

Injera is not always pure teff. Barley, finger millet, sorghum, wheat, maize and rice are also used, alone or mixed with teff, depending on local tradition and price. In the lowlands injera is often made with sorghum, and in the highlands barley is common. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera)

## How Injera Is Made

Making injera takes several days and relies on knowledge passed down in households rather than written recipes. [Neela & Fanta (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42779-020-00069-x) Teff flour is mixed with water and fermented with _ersho_, a clear yellow liquid saved from the surface of a previous batch. The primary fermentation lasts roughly 24 to 72 hours, depending on temperature, altitude, the amount of starter and the container. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera) Yeasts and lactic acid bacteria do the work, and they give injera its sour taste. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera)

A small portion of the fermented dough is then boiled with water to make _absit_, a cooked paste that acts as a binder. Once it has cooled, the absit is stirred back into the dough for a short second fermentation of 30 to 120 minutes, which produces gas bubbles. Injera made without absit is soft and thin and lacks the characteristic holes. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera)

The thinned batter is poured in a spiral, from the outside inward, onto a large round clay griddle called a _mitad_, heated to about 90–95 °C, and covered with a lid. It cooks in two to four minutes. Because it is never flipped, the bottom stays smooth while steam raises the bubbles on top that Ethiopians call "eyes". Today the most common mitad is electric, with a clay plate and a hinged aluminum lid. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera)

## Injera at the Table

Injera is stored in a _mesob_, a cylindrical woven basket. A picture of a mesob appears on the 10-birr banknote. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera) On fast days, when Orthodox Christians avoid animal products, injera carries lentils, chickpea _shiro_ and vegetable dishes. On feast days it carries meat stews. As a Saveur report on an Easter meal in Addis Ababa put it, injera is "omnipresent on the Ethiopian table", but what is served on it "changes drastically between fasting and days of breaking fast". [Saveur: This Epic Meat Feast Is How Ethiopians Celebrate Easter](https://www.saveur.com/meat-feast-ethiopian-easter/)

The shared platter has its own etiquette. The best-known custom is _gursha_ (ጉርሻ): a host or companion places a handful of injera and stew directly into another diner's mouth. Hagar Salamon of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem describes gursha as a "dominant gesture" of Ethiopian hospitality that carries both secular and religious meanings. [Salamon, Northeast African Studies (2020)](https://doi.org/10.14321/nortafristud.19.2.0001) Injera also marks life events. It features at religious and national holidays, weddings and birthdays, and at funerals people from outside the family carry mesobs full of injera to the mourners. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera)

## Teff in the Economy

Two changes have reshaped the teff market in recent decades. Inside Ethiopia, IFPRI researchers found that farmers increasingly used chemical fertilizer, improved varieties and herbicides, especially near cities. Urban buyers also paid more for convenience, which helped give rise to a sizable injera food-service industry. [Minten et al. (2013)](https://hdl.handle.net/10568/153504)

Abroad, teff became fashionable as a gluten-free grain. In 2006 the Ethiopian government banned exports of raw teff, fearing that foreign demand would push up domestic prices. Processed injera could still be exported, largely to the diaspora. [Wikipedia: Teff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teff) The ban was lifted in 2015, according to Quartz; other accounts describe the 2015 change as partial. [Quartz: Ethiopia's teff flour is no longer patented as a Dutch invention](https://qz.com/africa/1545111/ethiopias-teff-flour-is-no-longer-patented-as-a-dutch-invention) [Wikipedia: Teff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teff)

Ownership of the grain was also disputed. A European patent filed in 2003 listed a Dutchman, Jans Roosjen, as the inventor of a way of processing teff flour. In November 2018 a Dutch court ruled that the patent lacked inventiveness. The verdict became final in 2019 when no appeal was filed, and the Ethiopian embassy in the Netherlands welcomed it. [Quartz](https://qz.com/africa/1545111/ethiopias-teff-flour-is-no-longer-patented-as-a-dutch-invention)

The stews served on injera are a world of their own; the most prestigious of them is described in [[doro-wat-and-festive-dining]].
