# Fasting Food and the Beyaynetu Platter

## 30-second overview

For Ethiopian Orthodox Christians, fasting (_tsom_, ጾም) is a way of eating as much as a religious duty. On fast days people avoid meat, milk, butter and eggs, and eat nothing before noon. [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church: Worship](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/ethiopian/worship.html) The result is a large repertoire of vegan dishes, usually served together on injera as _yetsom beyaynetu_ (የጾም በያይነቱ), the "fasting platter." This article explains the rules, the dishes, and what researchers have measured about how fasting changes diets and health.

## The rules

The church's own description says there are "approximately 250 fast days in the year," though not all are compulsory for everyone, and that the average person may fast about 180 days. Fasting means abstaining from meat and animal products, and "no food or drink is taken before noon, at the earliest"; even after noon "only a simple repast should be taken." Pregnant women, the seriously sick and travelers are exempt, as are children under seven from the obligatory fasts. [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church: Worship](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/ethiopian/worship.html) Wednesdays and Fridays are the regular fast days, and the longest fasts are the 55-day Lent and the 43-day Fast of the Prophets. [Bazzano et al., PLoS One 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/6281271/) For the full list of fasts and the feasts that end them, see [Ethiopian religious festivals](/en/culture/ethiopian-religious-festivals).

How many days people really fast is less certain than the church's figure. A 2021 PLoS One study of 870 milk-producing households near Addis Ababa, about 85 percent of them Orthodox, surveyed in early 2018, found that Orthodox adults fasted an average of about 140 days a year, with 5.2 days for children aged 5 to 6, 34.1 for children aged 7 to 9, 98.4 for adolescents and 141.7 for the elderly. Between 11 and 12 percent of Orthodox adults fasted on no official fast days. The authors note that other sources give 166 to 180, or 180 to 250 days. [D'Haene, Vandevelde and Minten, PLoS One 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659323) The study covers one region and an agricultural sample, so it is not a national figure.

## What is on the plate

Restaurants commonly offer a platter for fast days, and its name carries the idea. The older names for the dish were _nai tsom migbi_ ("fasting food") and _yetsom beyanetu_, according to a 2017 East Bay Express article on the history of the "veggie combo." [East Bay Express, 2017](https://eastbayexpress.com/?p=15704) Tasting Table glosses _beyaynetu_ as "a bit of every type" and says restaurants typically offer three to six options, often shiro, _misir wat_ (red lentil stew), _kik alicha_ (mild split-pea stew), _gomen_ (collard greens) and _karat alicha_. [Tasting Table, 2023](https://tastingtable.com/1388955/ethiopian-beyainatu-variety-dish) The East Bay Express piece lists a similar core of misir wot, kik alicha, gomen or _hamli_ and shiro, and notes that restaurants in Ethiopia and Eritrea often add more vegetable dishes than most overseas menus do. [East Bay Express](https://eastbayexpress.com/?p=15704)

Shiro is the best-known item. Wikipedia describes it as a stew from northern Ethiopia and southern Eritrea made from powdered chickpeas or broad bean meal, often with onion, garlic and, depending on region, ginger, tomato or chili. It is served on injera and is normally vegan, though some versions use spiced butter (_niter kibbeh_) or meat; the meat version is called _bozena shiro_. A bubbling version served in a small clay pot is _tegabino shiro_, and _shiro fit-fit_ is made with shredded injera. Wikipedia adds that shiro is a favorite for fasting periods such as the Orthodox Lent and Ramadan. [Wikipedia: Shiro](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiro_(food)>) The Muslim fast of Ramadan is therefore another reason these dishes are cooked in many Ethiopian homes. For the flatbread underneath them all, see [injera and teff](/en/food/injera-and-teff).

Two cautions about popular sources. Tasting Table says most Orthodox Christians in Ethiopia fast on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays; the church's own page names Wednesdays and Fridays, and this article follows the church. [Tasting Table](https://tastingtable.com/1388955/ethiopian-beyainatu-variety-dish); [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/ethiopian/worship.html) The East Bay Express article also quotes a chef who says customers "only order tsom food when it's tsom," a reminder that the fasting dishes are tied to the calendar and not eaten at random. [East Bay Express](https://eastbayexpress.com/?p=15704)

## Fasting and the dairy economy

Fasting reshapes markets as well as menus. The 2021 PLoS One study, "Fasting, food and farming," found that fasting reduces annual milk consumption nationally by about 12 percent. During long fasts, daily milk production fell by about one quarter on average and milk sales by about 28 percent, rising to about 80 percent for remote households. Farms processed up to 60 percent of their milk into storable butter and cheese. Prices fell by roughly 3 to 5 percent for liquid milk, 7 to 8 percent for butter and about 18 percent for cheese. [D'Haene et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659323) Children, who under seven are exempt, benefited: the youngest child's milk intake rose about 60 percent, roughly one extra liter, during a long fasting week. [D'Haene et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659323) These are survey results from households near Addis Ababa and may differ elsewhere.

## Fasting, health and children

Researchers have measured fasting as a natural experiment. A 2019 study in Scientific Reports compared 253 fasters with 451 non-fasters among 704 Jimma University employees during the Orthodox Lent of about 55 days, defining fasters as those who avoided animal-source foods and skipped breakfast at least until midday. Fasters had lower body fat (30.59 percent against 32.35), lower LDL cholesterol (89.55 against 97.98 mg/dl), higher HDL (68.29 against 57.24) and lower fasting blood sugar (95.11 against 100.14), while body mass index did not differ significantly. The authors caution that the study cannot separate the effect of skipping breakfast from that of avoiding animal foods, relies on self-reported fasting, and did not measure micronutrients or whether the effects last outside Lent. [Sinaga et al., Scientific Reports 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6861246)

The picture is more mixed for mothers and children. A qualitative PLoS One study in four regions found that meat is scarcer and more expensive during long fasts, that pregnant and breastfeeding women were often expected to fast in practice despite the church's exemption, and that some mothers withheld animal-source foods from children on fast days to avoid contaminating shared utensils. It cites earlier work linking Orthodox fasting seasons with lower dietary diversity in children; in one cited study, Orthodox children whose mothers withheld animal-source foods were 1.5 times less likely to meet the dietary-diversity recommendation. The authors stress the study is qualitative and did not measure individual religiosity, so doctrine and local custom cannot be fully separated. [Bazzano, Potts and Mulugeta, PLoS One 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/6281271/)

## Breaking the fast

Fasting ends in feasting. The Lenten fast is broken after midnight Mass on Easter, and meat dishes such as [doro wat](/en/food/doro-wat-and-festive-dining) return to the table. The timing of that midnight Mass and meal is described in the festivals article. The contrast between the two halves of the year is part of what gives each its flavor.

## Closing

Fasting cuisine in Ethiopia is an old, widely shared discipline that has shaped markets, households and menus. Its dishes are everyday food for millions, and research on it suggests the same practice can bring benefits to some bodies and risks to others.
