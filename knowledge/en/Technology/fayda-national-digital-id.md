# Fayda: Ethiopia's National Digital ID

## 30-second overview

Fayda (ፋይዳ) is Ethiopia's national digital identity system, run by the National ID Program (NIDP). Each registrant gets a 12-digit Fayda Identification Number, and enrollment captures all ten fingerprints, both irises and a facial photograph ([National ID Program: About Fayda](https://id.gov.et/about)). The system was established by a Digital ID Proclamation passed in March 2023 ([World Bank: press release, 13 December 2023](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2023/12/13/world-bank-supports-afe-ethiopias-digital-id-project-to-increase-access-to-services-and-economic-opportunities)). By 24 August 2026 the program reported 49.1 million enrollments ([Biometric Update: Fayda digital ID nears 50M enrollments](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202608/fayda-digital-id-nears-50m-enrollments-as-adoption-reshapes-ethiopias-economy)). The program is promoted as a foundation for banking and public services; rights groups and researchers have raised questions about privacy, exclusion and ethnic profiling. This article gives both sides and notes that most enrollment figures come from the program itself.

## What It Is and What It Collects

The NIDP site describes the vision as "an inclusive and secure digital identity to every resident." It says registration collects fingerprints of all ten fingers, iris scans of both eyes and a face photo, and requires copies of accepted proof-of-identity documents. The 12-digit number is sent to the registrant by SMS and can optionally be printed on a card. Registered partners can use authentication interfaces to verify a number against demographic or biometric data, and the system supports electronic know-your-customer checks ([NIDP](https://id.gov.et/about)). The page also still states a target of more than 70 million people "by the end of 2025," which shows it has not been updated to match current targets.

## Financing and Aims

The World Bank approved the Digital ID for Inclusion and Services Project on 13 December 2023, with 350 million dollars from the International Development Association, of which 50 million dollars is a grant through the window for host communities and refugees. It says millions of Ethiopians lack current forms of identification and that more than 3.5 million people had registered in pilots. The project aims to provide at least 90 million Ethiopians, plus refugees and migrants, with an ID, to improve access to financial services, social protection, jobs, health care and education, to reduce leakage and fraud in public programs, and to fund a Personal Data Protection Commission. The Bank lists safeguards including voluntary registration, data minimization, tokenization of sensitive data and stronger cybersecurity ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2023/12/13/world-bank-supports-afe-ethiopias-digital-id-project-to-increase-access-to-services-and-economic-opportunities)). These are the lender's stated aims, not independent findings on outcomes.

Reported figures show rapid growth, all from the program or its coverage:

([IDTechWire](https://idtechwire.com/ethiopias-digital-id-system-now-integrated-across-55-key-institutions/); [Biometric Update, March 2026](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202603/ethiopias-digital-id-joins-sovereign-wealth-fund-as-weekly-enrollments-reach-1m); [Biometric Update, August 2026](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202608/fayda-digital-id-nears-50m-enrollments-as-adoption-reshapes-ethiopias-economy))

The target is 90 million enrollments by 2027, according to the August 2026 report. That piece also cites more than 194 million electronic identity verifications and says the Prime Minister expects Fayda to contribute about 7 percent of GDP by 2030. The report relies mainly on officials and state broadcaster EBC and raises no criticism, so these claims are the government's. Biometric Update's March report says the program was moving from a project office toward a state-owned entity under Ethiopian Investment Holdings, with ID card costs expected to fall from 350 to 150 birr; the August report says the authority became a government-owned commercial enterprise, Faydaverse, in early August 2026.

## Concerns

A 2023 Thomson Reuters Foundation report quoted Omna Tigray's Jerusalem Girmay and the University of London researcher Eve Hayes de Kalaf, who warned that the ID could entrench discrimination against Tigrayans and other minorities and enable ethnic profiling through arrests and surveillance. Officials responded that Fayda does not display ethnicity, that registration was not mandatory and that the older kebele ID remained usable. The report adds that "a person's ethnic identity can be ascertained from their name," and quotes the program's legal policy adviser as saying mandatory enrollment could exclude people whose fingerprints are unreadable ([Context: Ethiopia digital ID prompts fears of ethnic profiling](https://www.context.news/surveillance/ethiopia-digital-id-prompts-fears-of-ethnic-profiling)). That reporting dates from February 2023, during the aftermath of the Tigray war, and the program has changed greatly since.

In 2022 the legal scholar Kebene Wodajo wrote that Ethiopia's protocol document lists only name, gender, address and date of birth as core data and says religion, ethnicity and place of birth would not be collected, but that these can be inferred from names, addresses and language; she noted that no data protection law existed yet and that service access was largely in English ([Wodajo: Digitalizing identity, Opinio Juris](https://opiniojuris.org/2022/02/10/digitalizing-identity-precautionary-thoughts-on-ethiopias-fayda-number/)). She argued for safeguards before rollout. We have not verified the current legal framework, including whether the Personal Data Protection Commission now operates, and later reporting seen in search results suggests Fayda is increasingly tied to banking; we have not checked that rule's text.

## Why It Matters

A foundational ID could make it easier to open accounts, receive payments and prove eligibility for services, which is why banking, telecommunications and government agencies are among the integrated institutions, according to IDTechWire. For the mobile-money side of the same picture, see [telebirr](/en/technology/telebirr-digital-payments). The same database, if poorly governed, could expand surveillance or exclude people who cannot enroll. The public evidence for the benefits is largely reported by the government and its financiers; the public evidence for the risks is largely from advocates and academics, and both depend on how the law is enforced.

## Closing

Fayda is a large, fast-growing public system whose benefits and risks are both documented mainly by interested parties. The most useful next sources would be an independent audit of enrollment numbers and a review of the enforcement of the data protection law.
