# ETRSS-1 and Ethiopia's Satellite Programme

## 30-second overview

On 20 December 2019 a Chinese Long March 4B rocket carried the first Ethiopian Remote Sensing Satellite, ETRSS-1, into orbit from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center ([China Daily: Ethiopia's first satellite launched](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/201912/20/WS5dfc5dbea310cf3e3557f874.html)). It made Ethiopia the eleventh African country with a satellite in space, according to AFP ([AFP via Phys.org: Ethiopia celebrates launch of first satellite](https://phys.org/news/2019-12-ethiopia-celebrates-satellite.pdf)). The satellite was designed and built in China and is meant to supply imagery for agriculture, water, disaster response and climate work. Ethiopia has since launched a second satellite, ET-SMART-RSS, and plans a further one in December 2026. The sources disagree on some technical details and on how the cost was shared, and we note those differences below.

## Why a Satellite

Ethiopia's agriculture and forests are vulnerable to flood, drought and other climate hazards, and AFP reported that the satellite would send data on the environment and weather patterns of the Horn of Africa. The innovation and technology minister, Getahun Mekuria, said it would save money because Ethiopia would no longer need to pay for remote-sensing data from foreign satellites ([AFP via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-12-ethiopia-celebrates-satellite.pdf)). China Daily lists its uses as multispectral data for agriculture, water resource surveys, disaster prevention and relief, and climate change research ([China Daily](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/201912/20/WS5dfc5dbea310cf3e3557f874.html)). Wikipedia lists a spatial resolution of 13.7 meters and four spectral bands, which fits a medium-resolution crop and land-cover satellite, though that page cites only secondary sources ([Wikipedia: ETRSS-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETRSS-1)).

## Who Built It and Who Paid

China Daily says the China Academy of Space Technology designed and built the satellite, with Cui Yufu as chief designer, that the two governments signed the agreement in October 2016, and that the Chinese government covered the costs ([China Daily](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/201912/20/WS5dfc5dbea310cf3e3557f874.html)). AFP gives the cost as about 8 million dollars, "mostly" covered by China, according to an official who spoke anonymously, and says 21 Ethiopian scientists helped with the development, citing the specialist site africanews.space ([AFP via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-12-ethiopia-celebrates-satellite.pdf)). Space in Africa gives a finer split: about 8 million dollars including the ground station, with China providing 6 million and Ethiopia 2 million for ground facilities at the Entoto Space Observatory near Addis Ababa ([Space in Africa: Ethiopia launches first satellite named ETRSS-1](https://spaceinafrica.com/?p=3385)). The three accounts agree that China bore most of the cost, but the official breakdown has not been published in anything we could read.

## The Launch

The launch was planned for 17 December and delayed three days by weather, and ETRSS-1 flew as a secondary payload with the CBERS-4A satellite as the primary payload, according to Space in Africa. Its mass is given as 70 kilograms there and by AFP, as 65 kilograms by China Daily, and as 64 to 65 kilograms by Wikipedia. Altitude is reported as about 600 kilometers by China Daily and about 700 by Wikipedia, and China Daily says it was expected to operate at least two years. We do not choose between these figures. At the Entoto Observatory and Research Centre outside Addis Ababa, officials and scientists watched a live broadcast, and Deputy Prime Minister Demeke Mekonnen called it "a foundation for our historic journey to prosperity" ([AFP via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-12-ethiopia-celebrates-satellite.pdf)).

AFP also placed it in context: it was the eighth launch of an African satellite in 2019, a record, and one of 41 African satellites launched in total, none from African soil. Egypt was first, in 1998 ([AFP via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-12-ethiopia-celebrates-satellite.pdf)).

## The People and the Institute

AFP reports that Ethiopia's space effort began with private enthusiasts who formed the Ethiopian Space Science Society in 2004; one member, Paulos Alemayehu, recalled that many were at first skeptical, quoting the objection "In a country where tap water is not even reaching the upper stories of buildings, what the hell are you talking about?" It credits Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, then minister of science and technology, with encouraging the partnership with China in 2015 and 2016, and says the government established the Ethiopian Space Science and Technology Institute in 2016 ([AFP via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-12-ethiopia-celebrates-satellite.pdf)). The institute now appears in official reports as the Space Science and Geospatial Institute (SSGI). Wikipedia says it spent several months calibrating the satellite and released the first images in February 2020 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETRSS-1)).

## What Came After

Space in Africa reported in March 2025 that Ethiopia's second satellite was ET-SMART-RSS, launched in 2020, and that a further satellite, ETRSS-2, was being built with the Shanghai Engineering Center for Microsatellites; it has a 0.5-meter resolution and a five-year design life, and the date "may be subject to adjustment" ([Space in Africa: Ethiopia gives updates on its third satellite project](https://spaceinafrica.com/2025/03/12/ethiopia-gives-updates-on-its-third-satellite-project/)). The Ethiopian News Agency reported the contract signing with the Shanghai center on 26 November 2024, with development planned over 18 months and SSGI's director general, Abdissa Yilma, calling space science "not a luxury rather an essential area" ([ENA: Agreement signed with Shanghai Microsatellites to launch 2nd Ethiopian satellite](https://www.ena.et/web/eng/w/eng_5523992)). A January 2026 ENA report, distributed by AllAfrica, says both earlier satellites, ET-RSS1 and ET-SMART-RSS, have completed their operational lifespans, and that the next one is planned for December 2026 ([ENA via AllAfrica: Ethiopian satellites drive socio-economic gains](https://allafrica.com/stories/202601090294.html)). The same reports call the new satellite both the "second" and the "third" Ethiopian satellite, depending on whether ET-SMART-RSS is counted. We found no confirmation that it has launched.

## Assessment

The cited sources show a state-led programme built on Chinese technology and finance, with Ethiopian staff in training and data roles. They do not show how widely ETRSS-1 imagery was used, whether it saved money as the minister predicted, or how many Ethiopian engineers worked on the design rather than the data. The SSGI director general said the programme built "institutional capacity in satellite data reception, analysis, and management" ([ENA via AllAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202601090294.html)), which is an official claim we could not test.

## Closing

ETRSS-1 gave Ethiopia its first orbital asset and a seat among African space nations. The most useful future additions would be independent assessments of data use and a post-launch report on the next satellite.
