# The Ge'ez Script (Fidel): An African Abugida with Two Thousand Years of Writing

**In 30 seconds.** The script that Ethiopians call _fidel_ (ፊደል) is the writing system of Amharic and Tigrinya and the traditional script of the liturgical language Ge'ez (ግዕዝ). It is an abugida: each sign stands for a consonant plus a vowel, and the vowel is shown by a small change to the base shape. It grew out of a South Arabian consonant script and took its present form in the 4th century CE. It has one of the longest documented histories of any writing system native to Africa. The sections below cover how it works, how it developed, and how it is used today.

## How the script works

The Ethiopian script is written left to right, and each basic sign carries a consonant and a vowel. A leading linguistics institute in Paris, INALCO, explains that the consonants became basic syllabographs with the vowel _ä_, and that diacritical marks added to the base shape write the other six vowels, _u_, _i_, _a_, _e_, _ǝ_ and _o_. For example, the consonant በ is _bä_, ቡ is _bu_, ቢ is _bi_, ባ is _ba_, ቤ is _be_, ብ is _bǝ_ and ቦ is _bo_. The vowel sequence is fixed, and the "orders" are named with Ge'ez ordinal numbers, so that the second order is _kaʕǝb_. [INALCO: Writing in Amharic](https://inalco.fr/en/writing-amharic) The word _abugida_ itself comes from the first signs of an alphabet book arranged in a different order, and _fidel_ is the term for the arrangement of signs in a primer. [INALCO](https://inalco.fr/en/writing-amharic)

The Unicode Standard treats the script as a grid: the main block of the code space, U+1200 to U+1357, is a matrix of 43 consonants crossed with 8 vowels, giving 344 conceptual syllables, most of which are used. It states that the script has more than 300 characters, which is why keyboard input usually works with a smaller set of codes. [Unicode Standard 15.0, chapter 19: Africa](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode15.0.0/ch19.pdf) The Unicode text also notes that the basic forms are sometimes modified to write additional languages.

Other features include numerals derived from Greek letters, which have no sign for zero and are written by adding values (for example 123 is ፻፳፫), and a word divider that was originally a vertical bar and later a colon-like mark; a modern full stop is ። and today the space has often replaced the word divider. [INALCO](https://inalco.fr/en/writing-amharic) INALCO adds that the Ethiopian script is the only abugida among the Semitic scripts.

## Origins

The earliest writing in Ethiopia and Eritrea, according to INALCO, consists of pseudo-Sabaean inscriptions in a consonant script from South Arabia from the 8th or 7th centuries BCE, probably made by Ethiosemitic speakers for whom Sabaean was a learned language. The Ethiopian consonant script (abjad) that first appeared to write Ge'ez between the 1st and 3rd centuries CE is described as clearly a modification of the South Arabian one, which has 29 consonant signs. [INALCO](https://inalco.fr/en/writing-amharic) Other sources, such as [Omniglot](https://www.omniglot.com/writing/ethiopic.htm), give a different date for the earliest inscriptions, so the starting point depends on the source and on how one defines "the script". We follow INALCO here because it is a university source.

The change to an abugida happened in the 4th century CE. INALCO states that in that century the script "switched from an abjad to an abugida", and adds that Greek influence probably triggered further changes: the use of Greek letters as number signs, the switch from boustrophedon to left-to-right writing, and probably the invention of two extra signs, ፐ _pä_ and ጰ _p'ä_. [INALCO](https://inalco.fr/en/writing-amharic) A Wikipedia summary notes that the first completely vocalized texts known are inscriptions of King Ezana of Aksum, and that a vocalized letter appears on a coin of his predecessor Wazeba. It also records the church tradition that credits the present vowel system to a team of Aksumite scholars led by Frumentius, the missionary said to have converted Ezana. [Wikipedia: Geʽez script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge%CA%BDez_script) Scholars disagree on whether Indian (Brahmic) scripts influenced the vowel marking: INALCO mentions Brahmi inscriptions and Ethiopian graffiti found in a cave on Socotra, but also notes it is argued that the Ethiopian script was an independent innovation. [INALCO](https://inalco.fr/en/writing-amharic) For the kingdom in which these developments took place, see [the Aksumite Empire](/en/history/aksumite-empire).

## From Ge'ez to Amharic and other languages

Ge'ez is the oldest known Ethiosemitic language. INALCO says it ceased to be spoken in the second half of the first millennium CE but remains the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and that until the late 19th century it was the only language written at the royal court, with Amharic serving as lingua franca. Amharic took over the script in the 13th or 14th century but was written only sporadically until the early 20th century, when it became the language of primary-school instruction and the national language. [INALCO](https://inalco.fr/en/writing-amharic)

The adaptation of the script to Amharic produced new signs: INALCO notes that the invention of signs for labialised consonants and for palatal or spirantised consonants followed the vocalisation and seems to be due to the adaptation of the script to write Amharic. The Unicode Standard names Amharic, Tigre and Oromo among the languages for which the script has been adopted for modern use. [Unicode Standard 15.0](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode15.0.0/ch19.pdf)

## A manuscript culture

The script is bound up with a long manuscript tradition. A study of the monastic collection at May Wäyni in Tigray, by Jacek Tomaszewski and Michael Gervers, notes that there are thousands of churches in Ethiopia and that almost every one stores parchment manuscripts containing ancient and sometimes unknown religious texts. The May Wäyni collection of 91 items was digitised in 2013 through the British Library's Endangered Archives Programme; the authors say a provincial church library typically holds about forty manuscripts and that most of the May Wäyni books appear to have been copied in the 18th and 19th centuries. [Tomaszewski and Gervers: Technological aspects of the monastic manuscript collection at May Wäyni, Ethiopia (Open Book Publishers)](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0052/4.Gervers.xhtml)

Among the oldest surviving examples are the Garima Gospels, two illuminated Gospel books in Ge'ez kept at the Abba Garima monastery. In 2010 reports of radiocarbon testing at Oxford said that parchment samples dated them to somewhere between 330 and 650 CE, much earlier than had been assumed. [Medievalists.net: Garima Gospels found to be oldest surviving Christian illustrated manuscripts](https://www.medievalists.net/2010/07/garima-gospels-found-to-be-oldest-surviving-christian-illustrated-manuscripts/) A Wikipedia entry gives narrower ranges of about 390 to 570 for Garima 2 and 530 to 660 for Garima 1, and describes Garima 2 as the earliest surviving complete illuminated Christian manuscript, a claim that depends on the dating accepted. [Wikipedia: Garima Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garima_Gospels)

## Debates about the script

The script also features in debates about other Ethiopian languages. For Afaan Oromo, the Ge'ez script was among the scripts used before the Latin-based _Qubee_ alphabet became the official writing system in 1991. A University of Kent doctoral thesis on the development of Oromo writing treats this history in detail, including the arguments for and against different scripts. [Teferi Degeneh Bijiga, University of Kent (2015): The Development of Oromo Writing System](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335) See [Afaan Oromo and the Qubee alphabet](/en/language/afaan-oromo-and-the-qubee-alphabet).

## Closing

The fidel is both a working script in everyday use and a record of how a writing system can be reshaped over centuries. Dates for its earliest stages vary by source, and the account above reflects the reading we could verify.
