# Afaan Oromo and the Qubee Alphabet: How the Largest Language of Ethiopia Found Its Script

**In 30 seconds.** Afaan Oromo (Oromiffa) is a Cushitic language and, by the 2007 census, the most widely spoken mother tongue in Ethiopia. For well over a century it was written in several scripts by missionaries, religious scholars and activists, but it had no official written standard. On 3 November 1991, after the fall of the Derg, a gathering of more than a thousand Oromo participants chose the Latin alphabet, now called _Qubee_. Since then Afaan Oromo has become a language of schools, books and regional administration in Oromia, and the choice of script remains a subject of public debate.

## A very widely spoken language

The 2007 national census recorded 33.8% of Ethiopians as Oromo mother-tongue speakers, ahead of Amharic at 29.3%, as summarised in a Wikipedia article on the languages of Ethiopia. [Wikipedia: Languages of Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ethiopia) Other sources use different measures. Omniglot gives a figure of about 30 million speakers across Ethiopia, Kenya, Somalia and Egypt and says the Oromo account for more than 40% of Ethiopia's population, and a 2015 University of Kent thesis says Oromo is believed to be spoken by about 40% of Ethiopians. [Omniglot: Oromo](https://omniglot.com/writing/oromo.htm) [Teferi Degeneh Bijiga, University of Kent (2015): The Development of Oromo Writing System](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335) The 2007 census count is the only one of these tied to a named enumeration, so we prefer it, but ethnic identity and mother tongue are different measures and the estimates should be read as a range of roughly one third to two fifths.

## Many scripts before Qubee

Oromo has a strong oral tradition, and several scripts were tried for it. A 2022 article in the _Journal of Ethiopian Studies_ lists the Ethiopic (Ge'ez) script, Arabic, 'Ajami, Sheikh Bakri Sapalo's script and Latin among the scripts used, together with the Walābū script of Sheikh Kemal Adem. [Nuraddin Aman, Journal of Ethiopian Studies (2022): An Investigation into the Walābū Oromo Script Devised by Sheikh Kemal Adem](https://ejol.aau.edu.et/index.php/JES/article/view/8460)

- **Arabic.** According to the same author, Muslim Oromo in Harar, Wollo, Jimma, Bale and Arsi wrote Afaan Oromo in the Arabic alphabet with added marks for sounds Arabic lacks, mainly for religious poems and teaching material. [Aman (2022), full text](https://ejol.aau.edu.et/index.php/JES/article/download/8460/6785/14399)
- **Latin.** The same article says that Oromo literature in Latin script began to spread from the 1840s through European missionaries in western Oromia. [Aman (2022)](https://ejol.aau.edu.et/index.php/JES/article/download/8460/6785/14399)
- **Home-made scripts.** The Kent thesis describes the script devised in the 1950s by Sheikh Bakri Sapalo, who it says was forced to flee to Somalia and died there in 1980 at about 85. [Teferi (2015)](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335) Aman documents a second script, devised in Bale in the early 1960s by Sheikh Kemal Adem, in which Kemal produced four manuscripts. [Aman (2022)](https://ejol.aau.edu.et/index.php/JES/article/view/8460)

For the Ge'ez script, which was also used for Oromo, see [the Ge'ez script (fidel)](/en/language/geez-script-fidel).

## Policy before 1991

Historians describe a long period in which languages other than Amharic received little official support. The Kent thesis argues that under Emperor Haile Selassie's government Oromo was not allowed to be written even in the Ge'ez script, that the Derg after 1974 gave it only a limited opportunity, and that writing in Afaan Oromo in any script was prohibited or seriously discouraged until the Derg's fall in 1991. [Teferi (2015)](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335) Omniglot summarises the same period differently, saying that until the 1970s Oromo was written in either Ge'ez or Latin, and that between 1974 and 1991 writing in any script was forbidden, though limited use of Ge'ez was allowed. [Omniglot](https://omniglot.com/writing/oromo.htm) The two accounts differ in detail and in the extent of the restrictions, so the period is best described as one of severe limits that varied by time and place.

## The decision for Latin

The Kent thesis traces the Latin choice to the Oromo Liberation Front (OLF), formed in 1973. The OLF started using Afaan Oromo for its own business and adopted the Roman script, which it required officials and fighters to learn, after consultations with Oromo and non-Oromo scholars; it drew on the experience of Somali, Hausa and Swahili, which were already written in Latin. [Teferi (2015)](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335) The thesis says the word _qubee_ was in clandestine use in Oromia and openly in the diaspora from at least the 1970s, and that dictionaries originally defined it as "ring", not as an alphabet. [Teferi (2015)](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335)

Then, on 3 November 1991, the OLF convened a conference of Oromo scholars. Tilahun Gamta, who attended, reported that over 1,000 men and women there unanimously decided in favour of the Latin alphabet. [Teferi (2015)](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335) Omniglot gives the same date and number. [Omniglot](https://omniglot.com/writing/oromo.htm)

## How Qubee works

Qubee uses the letters of the Roman alphabet without added marks, apart from the apostrophe; the thesis says 25 letters and the apostrophe were adopted, and that only "v" is not used. Several sounds are written with digraphs: _ch_, _dh_, _ny_, _ph_ and _sh_. The ejective consonants take single letters, _c_, _x_ and _q_ in the thesis's table, with examples such as _cabbii_ ("snow"), _fixuu_ ("to finish") and _marqaa_ ("porridge"). Long vowels and double consonants are written by doubling the letter, so _aadaa_ ("culture") and _eeboo_ ("spear") contrast with their short forms, and _samuu_ ("to rot") differs from _sammuu_ ("brain"). [Teferi (2015)](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335)

The same thesis criticises the system for inconsistency. It notes that some ejectives are written with single letters and others with digraphs, that "x" has no obvious link to the sound it represents, and that doubling can make writing slower, but it also explains why single letters were preferred for simplicity, and why "q" matches Somali practice. [Teferi (2015)](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335) These are the observations of one researcher and the orthography is used as adopted.

## Since 1991

The thesis, written around 2015, reports that Afaan Oromo is taught as a subject and used as a medium of instruction in primary and junior secondary schools in Oromia, is an official language of Oromia, and is studied at Addis Ababa University and other universities. It estimates that more books were published in Oromo in the two decades after 1991 than in the preceding century, and notes that newspapers such as _Urjii_ and _Bariisa_ switched from Ge'ez to Qubee in the early 1990s. It also records that many independent Oromo newspapers and magazines were later closed and some journalists imprisoned or exiled, according to the author. [Teferi (2015)](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/id/document/39335) These statements are as of the thesis date and should not be read as describing the situation today.

The choice of script still prompts discussion. An essay published in 2020 by the Oromo-oriented site Curate Oromia, written by a historian who defends Qubee, says that some people are petitioning the regional governments of Oromia and Amhara to replace it with the Ge'ez script. [Guluma Gemeda, Curate Oromia (2020): The History and Politics of the Qubee Alphabet](https://curateoromia.com/the-history-and-politics-of-the-qubee-alphabet/) That source is an advocacy piece rather than a neutral account, and we cite it only as evidence that the debate continues. The Unicode Standard, for its part, lists Oromo among the languages that have used the Ethiopic script. [Unicode Standard 15.0, chapter 19](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode15.0.0/ch19.pdf)

## Closing

Afaan Oromo's script history is a case of a large language gaining a standard written form late and quickly. The facts about the 1991 decision are well documented; the history before it and the debate after it are told differently by different authors.
