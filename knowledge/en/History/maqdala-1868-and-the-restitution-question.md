# Maqdala 1868: Emperor Tewodros II, the British Expedition and the Restitution Question

**In 30 seconds.** In 1868 Britain sent a large expedition from India to free a group of European captives held by Emperor Tewodros II (ዳግማዊ ቴዎድሮስ). On 13 April its troops stormed his fortress at Maqdala (መቅደላ) in the northern highlands. Tewodros killed himself, and the army carried off manuscripts, church treasures and royal belongings, many of which were sold at an army "prize" auction. Parts of this collection are still in British museums, a handful of objects have gone back to Ethiopia, and the question of returning the rest remains open.

## Tewodros and the road to Maqdala

Tewodros was crowned emperor in 1855. The Victoria and Albert Museum (V&A) records that he tried to win an alliance with Britain and wrote to Queen Victoria in 1857 and 1862 asking for military help. When the letters went unanswered, he took around 30 European diplomats and missionaries hostage in 1863. [V&A: Ethiopian collections at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/ethiopian-collections-at-the-vampa) Aregawi Berhe describes Tewodros as a ruler who set out to build a unified, modern Ethiopia and who, when the British besieged his mountain fortress, chose to take his own life rather than surrender; Berhe dates the death to 10 April, whereas the V&A and the Wikipedia account of the campaign place it during the 13 April assault. [Aregawi Berhe: Revisiting resistance in Italian-occupied Ethiopia (Leiden University, 2003)](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

The British government's response was a major military operation. Lieutenant General Sir Robert Napier led a force launched from Bombay in October 1867. Sources give slightly different numbers: the V&A's 2026 article speaks of around 12,000 British and Indian troops, while its 2018 blog post counts 13,000 troops with 26,000 camp followers and 40,000 animals. [V&A: Maqdala 1868](https://www.vam.ac.uk/content/articles/t/treasures-from-ethiopia/) The scale reflects the difficulty of the task: the expedition had to build a railway and landing facilities on the Red Sea coast, and even brought trained elephants from India to carry heavy guns. [Wikipedia: Expedition to Abyssinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expedition_to_Abyssinia)

## The assault, 13 April 1868

The expedition reached Maqdala in April 1868. According to the V&A, the British army's overwhelming firepower caused heavy casualties on the Ethiopian side while British losses were almost none. On 13 April Napier's forces launched the final attack, Tewodros's armies were completely defeated, and the emperor took his own life. [V&A: Ethiopian collections at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/ethiopian-collections-at-the-vampa) The Wikipedia account of the campaign adds that the British found Tewodros dead inside the second gate, that the European hostages had been released in the preceding days, and that, according to its sources, Ethiopian captives held at the fortress were mutilated and thrown over the precipice. [Wikipedia: Expedition to Abyssinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expedition_to_Abyssinia)

## The looting and the prize auction

After the battle the British army ransacked the fortress and the surrounding area, taking Ethiopian manuscripts, sacred objects of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church and many other valuables. The loot was carried to the Talanta plain, about ten miles away, where a week later the army held an officially sanctioned "prize" auction to raise money that was then shared among officers and troops. [V&A: Ethiopian collections at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/ethiopian-collections-at-the-vampa) The British Museum had sent a manuscripts curator, Richard Holmes, with the expedition, and the museum's own official history, quoted by The Art Newspaper, calls the episode "one of the less glorious" in its past. [The Art Newspaper (2005): UK museums face controversial Ethiopian legacy](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2005/10/01/uk-museums-face-controversial-ethiopian-legacy-the-maqdala-materials-at-the-british-museum-the-vanda-and-the-british-library)

Some of the best-known objects came from Gondar rather than from Tewodros's own court. A gold crown and a gold chalice now at the V&A were probably made in Gondar around 1735–1740 for the church of Our Lady of Qwesqwam, as gifts from Emperor Iyyasu II and his mother, Empress Mentewwab. Tewodros took them from churches during his campaigns, especially around Gondar, to furnish his own treasury and church at Maqdala. [V&A: Ethiopian collections at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/ethiopian-collections-at-the-vampa) The V&A notes that the crown was once thought to be Tewodros's own, but recent scholarship suggests it was commissioned in the 1740s. [V&A: Maqdala 1868](https://www.vam.ac.uk/content/articles/t/treasures-from-ethiopia/)

## The fate of the family

Tewodros's widow, Queen Terunesh, died a month after her husband on the journey toward her home province of Semien. Her seven-year-old son, Prince Alemayehu, was taken to Britain under the guardianship of Captain Tristram Speedy. The V&A reports his death in 1879 at the age of 18 or 19, depending on which of its own articles one reads, and a photograph of Alemayehu and Speedy by Julia Margaret Cameron is held in its collection. [V&A: Maqdala 1868](https://www.vam.ac.uk/content/articles/t/treasures-from-ethiopia/) [V&A: Ethiopian collections at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/ethiopian-collections-at-the-vampa)

## Contemporary criticism

The taking of the treasures was criticised at the time. The V&A quotes Prime Minister William Gladstone, who in a parliamentary debate in 1871 said he "deeply lamented" that the gold crown and chalice had been brought away by a British army, and urged that they be held "only until they could be restored". [V&A: Maqdala 1868](https://www.vam.ac.uk/content/articles/t/treasures-from-ethiopia/) The objects were not returned.

## Where the objects are today

The V&A says it holds around 90 objects from Ethiopia, most linked to the 1867–68 expedition. [V&A: Ethiopian collections at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/ethiopian-collections-at-the-vampa) The British Museum holds eleven _tabots_, wooden tablets that Ethiopian Orthodox believers regard as representing the Ark of the Covenant and that may be seen only by senior clergy; ten of them are part of the Maqdala material, and The Art Newspaper reported in 2005 that the museum kept them out of public view in a sealed room. [The Art Newspaper (2005)](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2005/10/01/uk-museums-face-controversial-ethiopian-legacy-the-maqdala-materials-at-the-british-museum-the-vanda-and-the-british-library) Maqdala material is also in the British Library, and, as The Art Newspaper noted, in several other UK collections.

## The restitution debate

In April 2018, on the 150th anniversary of the battle, the V&A opened a free display of about twenty Maqdala objects, and its director, Tristram Hunt, said the museum would be ready to assist if Ethiopia wished to pursue a long-term loan of the items, in which case ownership would stay with the museum. The Art Newspaper described the offer as likely to put pressure on other UK institutions. [The Art Newspaper (2018): V&A opens dialogue on looted Ethiopian treasures](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2018/04/03/vanda-opens-dialogue-on-looted-ethiopian-treasures) Hunt wrote in the V&A's blog that the display was meant to start "an ongoing dialogue about the history of these objects and their place in our national collection today". [V&A: Maqdala 1868](https://www.vam.ac.uk/content/articles/t/treasures-from-ethiopia/)

Returns have so far been limited and have happened outside the museum route. In October 2024 an engraved leather-and-silver shield taken at Maqdala was repatriated to Ethiopia, after being withdrawn from an auction in February 2024 following a request from the Ethiopian government; it was to go on display at the National Museum of Ethiopia in Addis Ababa. [The Art Newspaper (2024): Maqdala shield to be repatriated to Ethiopia](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2024/10/07/maqbala-shield-repatriation-ethiopia-exhibition-toledo-museum) The V&A reports that in 2025 it hosted a doctoral placement to trace the origins of its Ethiopian objects, which identified several previously unrecognised Maqdala items. [V&A: Ethiopian collections at the V&A](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/ethiopian-collections-at-the-vampa)

Positions differ on what should happen to the rest. Museums point to their legal and custodial responsibilities and to loan proposals; Ethiopia's ambassador to the UK called in April 2018 for the objects to be repatriated rather than lent, according to a Hyperallergic report of that month. [Hyperallergic (2018): Ethiopia Calls on London Museum to Repatriate Objects Looted 150 Years Ago](https://hyperallergic.com/439456/ethiopia-repatriation-victoria-albert-museum-london-looted-objects/) None of the sources used here gives a complete inventory of everything taken in 1868, so totals for the number of objects vary by collection and are not stated in this article.

## Closing

Maqdala is remembered in Ethiopia as the end of an ambitious reign and, in Britain, increasingly as a case study in how museum collections were built. The facts of the assault are well documented. How the objects should be shared today is still a live negotiation.
