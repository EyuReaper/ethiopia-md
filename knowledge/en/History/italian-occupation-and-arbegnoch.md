# The Italian Occupation and the Arbegnoch Resistance, 1935–1941

**In 30 seconds.** Italy, under Mussolini, invaded Ethiopia on 3 October 1935, used chemical weapons against its armies, and entered Addis Ababa on 5 May 1936. Yet the occupation was never complete. From the first months, scattered bands of "Patriots" (አርበኞች, _Arbegnoch_) fought on in the countryside, backed by priests, women's networks and urban clandestine groups. Italy's entry into the Second World War in 1940 brought British support, and Addis Ababa was liberated on 6 April 1941. Casualty figures for the period are disputed, and the sources below disagree in places.

## The invasion

Italy had never accepted the defeat at Adwa in 1896 (see [the Battle of Adwa](/en/history/battle-of-adwa)). The historian Aregawi Berhe notes that Mussolini ordered military preparations against Ethiopia as early as 1925 and gave the plan his full attention from 1934. The border clash at Welwel (Wal Wal) in December 1934 was a pretext rather than the cause. [Aregawi Berhe: Revisiting resistance in Italian-occupied Ethiopia (Leiden University, 2003)](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

Italian forces crossed the border on 3 October 1935. Both countries were members of the League of Nations, and Italy's attack broke the 1928 Italo-Ethiopian Treaty of Friendship and its League obligations. [SIPRI: The use of chemical weapons in the 1935–36 Italo-Ethiopian War](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/Italo-Ethiopian-war.pdf) SIPRI gives a rough balance of about 110,000 Italian soldiers against some 800,000 Ethiopians, but most Ethiopian soldiers carried outdated rifles or traditional weapons, and Italy had tanks and a far stronger air force. [SIPRI](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/Italo-Ethiopian-war.pdf) Adwa fell on 6 October 1935 and Mekelle on 8 November. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

## Chemical warfare

When the advance slowed, Mussolini replaced General De Bono with Marshal Pietro Badoglio. Berhe records that Badoglio then dropped mustard-gas bombs on troops and civilian areas, although both Italy and Ethiopia were signatories of the 1925 Geneva Protocol, which banned such weapons. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915) SIPRI dates the first documented use to December 1935, after an Ethiopian offensive had cut some Italian supply lines, and lists attacks at the Takkaze valley, Shire and Maychew, with the last reported use in April 1936. It cites one Soviet estimate that 15,000 of 50,000 Ethiopian casualties came from chemical weapons, but also warns that, given exaggerated claims on both sides, "accurate numbers will probably never be ascertained". [SIPRI](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/Italo-Ethiopian-war.pdf) The role of the International Committee of the Red Cross in the affair is examined in a 1998 article in the _International Review of the Red Cross_. [Baudendistel: Force versus law (Cambridge Core)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-the-red-cross-1961-1997/article/force-versus-law-the-international-committee-of-the-red-cross-and-chemical-warfare-in-the-italoethiopian-war-19351936/364DE165BB03641DE46708B573B0A966)

## Defeat in the field and exile

The Emperor's main force made a stand at Maychew and Lake Ashenge on 31 March 1936 and was forced to retreat. Haile Selassie reached Addis Ababa on 30 April, left for exile two days later, and Italian troops entered the capital on 5 May. Mussolini declared Ethiopia part of the Italian Empire on 9 May. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915) On 30 June 1936 the Emperor spoke at the League of Nations in Geneva, becoming the first head of state to address the Assembly; Italian journalists whistled and shouted from the gallery. [Swissinfo: The day the League of Nations failed Ethiopia](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/history/the-day-the-league-of-nations-failed-ethiopia/91666107) Berhe describes the League's and the West's response to Ethiopia's pleas as indifference. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

## The Patriots

Berhe argues that conventional defeat marked the start, not the end, of the struggle. Five months after the fall of the capital, Badoglio and Graziani, citing the historian Alberto Sbacchi, held only about a third of the country, and Berhe concludes that Ethiopia "was thus never effectively occupied or colonized". [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

The resistance drew on an older tradition. Italians called the fighters _shifta_ (bandits), but in Ethiopian usage the term could mean someone who takes to the hills against an unjust authority, and being called a shifta by the occupier was an honour. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915) Leaders included regional nobles such as Ras Abebe Aregai and Dejazmach Balcha Safo around Addis Ababa, and women such as Kebedech Seyoum in Tigray. Berhe stresses that priests, peasants, students and women took part as well as aristocrats. The Ethiopian Women's Volunteer Service Association became a clandestine network that supplied bandages and ammunition and forged pass papers, and the Black Lion Organization of young educated Ethiopians in Addis Ababa had its own written constitution. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915) A Wikipedia survey adds that bands were small, averaging an estimated 400 to 500 members in 1938. [Wikipedia: Arbegnoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbegnoch)

The Orthodox Church was central. Berhe reports that Abune Petros, Bishop of Wollo, denounced the occupation in the streets of Addis Ababa and was shot by firing squad on 30 July 1936. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

There was no single command, and there were collaborators. Berhe describes how Italy exploited rivalries among regional leaders, Christian-Muslim tensions and southern resentment of the central state. Two rivals of the Emperor, Dejazmach Haile Selassie Gugsa and Ras Hailu Tekle Haimanot, cooperated for a time, but Mussolini's rule of "No power to the rases" left them disappointed. Berhe also notes that Eritreans deserted to the Ethiopian side or joined the Patriots. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915) The resistance also had an economic side: in the countryside many people refused Italian lire and kept using Maria Theresa thalers. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

## Yekatit 12 and Debre Libanos

On 19 February 1937 (Yekatit 12), two young Eritreans, Abraha Deboch and Moges Asgedom, threw grenades at a ceremony in Addis Ababa and wounded Marshal Graziani. Italian forces and militia then killed civilians for days. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915) Estimates of the dead differ widely: Ethiopian sources claimed about 30,000, other estimates range from roughly 1,400 to 6,000, and a 2017 study put the figure near 19,200. [Wikipedia: Yekatit 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yekatit_12) On 21 May 1937 troops under General Pietro Maletti executed 297 monks and 23 laymen at the monastery of Debre Libanos, followed three days later by 129 deacons, according to the account summarised on Wikipedia, which draws on Italian reports. [Wikipedia: Arbegnoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbegnoch)

Mussolini replaced Graziani at the end of 1937 with the Duke of Aosta, who tried negotiation and bribes, and then a harsher campaign under General Cavallero in 1938 to 1939, which built the chains of forts and roads that Berhe says are still visible. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

## Liberation

Italy's entry into the war on 10 June 1940 changed the situation. Haile Selassie went to Khartoum in July 1940 and crossed back into Ethiopia on 30 January 1941 with General Orde Wingate, declaring that there would be no reprisals against Italians. Addis Ababa was liberated on 6 April 1941, and the Emperor re-entered the city on 5 May 1941. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915) Berhe points out that the Patriots had created their own political momentum, with ideas about justice and political reform, but these were not taken up by the restored imperial government, and some leaders who dissented were removed. [Berhe](https://hdl.handle.net/1887/12915)

## What historians still debate

- **Scale of losses.** Chemical-weapon casualties, the Yekatit 12 dead and the total toll of the occupation are all uncertain, and sources range from low thousands to tens of thousands.
- **Role of outside help.** Berhe argues that the arrival of the British army and the defeat of Italy brought the resistance to an abrupt end, and that without that outside intervention it would probably have continued as long as the Italians stayed.
- **Unity.** Responses were not uniform: some nobles and communities collaborated, and others fought. Berhe treats this as part of a long tension between centre and periphery rather than simple opportunism.

## Closing

The occupation lasted five years and left a lasting memory of both atrocity and endurance. What most Ethiopians recall, Berhe writes, is "the horrific behaviour of the invaders" on one side and "the endurance and heroic struggle of the Patriots" on the other.
