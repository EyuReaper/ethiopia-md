# The Aksumite Empire: Ancient Ethiopia’s Global Mercantile Power

The Kingdom of Aksum (አክሱም, also spelled Axum) lasted from roughly the 1st to the 8th century CE. Its center was on the Tigray plateau of northern Ethiopia, and its reach extended across present-day Eritrea and, at times, southern Arabia. UNESCO describes it as the most powerful state between the Eastern Roman Empire and Persia, standing at the crossroads of Africa, Arabia and the Greco-Roman world. Its fleets controlled Red Sea trade through the port of Adulis. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/) Near the end of the 3rd century, the Persian prophet Mani, in the text known as the _Kephalaia_, listed Aksum as one of the four great kingdoms of the world, alongside Rome, Persia and China. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum) Aksum's coins, granite stelae, inscriptions and early adoption of Christianity remain the foundation of how Ethiopia remembers its ancient past.

## Adulis and the Red Sea Trade

The earliest written mention of Aksum is in the _Periplus of the Erythraean Sea_, a Greek merchants' guide to the Red Sea and Indian Ocean that probably dates to the mid-1st century CE. [Casson: The Periplus Maris Erythraei (Princeton University Press)](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400843206) [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum) It names Aksum and the port of Adulis as lying within the realm of a ruler called Zoskales, and it describes the region as a source of ivory and tortoiseshell. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum)

UNESCO notes that Aksum commanded the ivory trade with the Sudan region and controlled the inland routes of northeastern Africa as well as the sea lanes. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/) Classical writers list ivory, tortoiseshell and rhinoceros horn among its exports. Pliny the Elder adds hippopotamus hide, monkeys and enslaved people. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum) The trade ran from the Roman Mediterranean in the north to India and Sri Lanka in the east, and Aksum sat in the middle of it.

The geographer Karl Butzer's study of the site's soils and sediments suggests that the city itself began around 100 CE as a ceremonial center and grew to more than 10,000 people as a trading emporium. Wetter, more reliable spring rains in that period allowed two harvests a year without irrigation. [Butzer: Rise and Fall of Axum, Ethiopia (American Antiquity, 1981)](https://doi.org/10.2307/280596)

## Coinage

Aksum issued its own coins in gold, silver and bronze, starting with King Endubis (Endybis) around 270 CE and continuing until the first half of the 7th century. [Wikipedia: Aksumite currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksumite_currency) Endubis based the weight of his gold coins on the Roman _aureus_. Most legends were written in Greek, the language of Red Sea commerce, and Ge'ez gradually replaced Greek on the silver and bronze issues. Aksumite coins of Ezana and of his predecessor Aphilas have been found as far away as India. [Wikipedia: Aksumite currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksumite_currency)

Coins are also among the best evidence for Aksum's history. Coins were struck in the names of about 18 to 20 kings, yet only two of them, Ezana and Kaleb, are also confirmed by inscriptions. An estimated 98 percent of the ancient city remains unexcavated, so numismatists have built much of the royal chronology from the coins. [Wikipedia: Aksumite currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksumite_currency) [Hahn: Aksumite Numismatics, a Critical Survey of Recent Research (Revue numismatique, 2000)](https://www.persee.fr/doc/numi_0484-8942_2000_num_6_155_2289)

## Ezana and the Adoption of Christianity

King Ezana (ዔዛና) ruled in the 4th century. Early coins of his reign carry the pre-Christian disc-and-crescent symbol. Later issues replace it with a cross, which is believed to be the first appearance of the Christian cross on any coinage. [Wikipedia: Aksumite currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksumite_currency) According to the traditional account, Ezana was converted by his teacher Frumentius, who was in contact with the Church of Alexandria and was appointed bishop of Ethiopia around 330. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum)

Ezana's stone inscriptions are among the most important documents of the ancient Horn of Africa. Some are trilingual, written in Greek, Sabaean and Ge'ez, and record his military campaigns. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/) Later inscriptions open with a Christian formula. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum) The Ge'ez script used in them was later adapted to mark vowels, becoming the syllabary (_fidel_) still used to write Ge'ez, Amharic, Tigrinya and other languages. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum)

UNESCO notes that the introduction of Christianity in the 4th century led to the building of churches such as Saint Mary of Zion (ማርያም ጽዮን). The present church was rebuilt in the 17th century and, by Ethiopian Orthodox tradition, holds the Ark of the Covenant. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/) That claim is a matter of faith: no outside observer has been allowed to examine the object.

## The Stelae

Aksum's best-known monuments are its granite stelae, which marked royal and elite tombs. UNESCO dates the surviving examples in the town to the 3rd and 4th centuries. The finest are carved to look like multi-story buildings, with false doors, windows and beam ends in stone. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/)

- **The Great Stela**, about 33 meters long, lies where it fell, possibly while it was being raised. UNESCO calls it perhaps the largest monolithic stela ancient people ever attempted to erect. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/)
- **The tallest stela still standing**, often called King Ezana's Stele, is carved as a nine-story building. UNESCO gives its height as over 23 meters, while other sources give about 21 meters. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/) [Wikipedia: King Ezana's Stele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Ezana%27s_Stele)
- **The Obelisk of Aksum**, about 24 meters tall and weighing about 160 tonnes, was taken to Rome in 1937 on Mussolini's orders during the Italian occupation. [Al Jazeera: Ethiopia gets back first bit of obelisk](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2005/4/19/ethiopia-gets-back-first-bit-of-obelisk) It was flown back to Aksum in three sections in April 2005 and re-erected in 2008. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/)

The ruins of Aksum were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1980. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/)

## Arabia, Decline and Legacy

Aksum's power across the Red Sea peaked under King Kaleb (c. 514 to 542). At the request of the Byzantine emperor, Kaleb invaded the Himyarite kingdom in Yemen after its king, Dhu Nuwas, persecuted Christians there. Aksum lost the territory again within a few years. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum) In 615, early Muslims fleeing persecution in Mecca found refuge in the Aksumite kingdom, an episode remembered in Islamic history as the First Hijra. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum)

The causes of decline are debated. Explanations include the loss of Red Sea trade to Persian and later Arab-Islamic powers, Beja migration into the northern plateau and environmental stress. [Wikipedia: Kingdom of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aksum) Butzer's geo-archaeological reading is that trade declined after about 600 and Aksum was effectively cut off from the sea by about 715. Heavy land use and more erratic rains then degraded the soils in the 7th and 8th centuries. The city was largely abandoned by about 800, and power shifted to the more fertile lands of central Ethiopia. [Butzer](https://doi.org/10.2307/280596) UNESCO places Aksum's political decline in the 10th century, yet Ethiopian emperors continued to be crowned there long afterward. [UNESCO: Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15/)
