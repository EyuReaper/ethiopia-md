# Traditional Ethiopian Instruments: Masenqo, Krar, and Washint

Long before the vibraphone and the electric organ arrived in Addis Ababa, the music of the Ethiopian highlands was carried by a small family of instruments: the one-string fiddle called the _masenqo_ (ማሲንቆ), the lyres called _krar_ (ክራር) and _begena_ (በገና), the _washint_ (ዋሽንት) flute and the _kebero_ (ከበሮ) drum. Two of them, the masenqo and the krar, are the tools of the _azmari_, the poet-musicians who sing improvised, often double-edged verse at weddings, in hotels and in bars. [The Musical Quarterly: Ashenafi Kebede, "The Azmari, Poet-Musician of Ethiopia" (1975)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mq/lxi.1.47) [PAM: A brief survey of African harps](https://pan-african-music.com/en/survey-of-african-harps/) The begena and the kebero belong to religious music, which the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church traces to Saint Yared in 6th-century Aksum. [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church: Church music](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/church/music.html) This article covers the instruments of the Christian highlands; Ethiopia's many other communities have their own instrumental traditions, which deserve their own articles.

## The Masenqo: One String and a Bow

The masenqo is a bowed spike fiddle. A museum example at London's Horniman Museum, made in Ethiopia around 1940, shows the typical construction: a wooden column runs through a lozenge-shaped (diamond-shaped) resonator made of planks joined with twists of wire and covered with hide. Its single string is made of several strands of horsehair, which pass over a bridge cut from an angled twig, and it is played with a bow. [Horniman Museum: Masenqo and bow](https://www.horniman.ac.uk/object/M23.2.65/5)

The instrument is closely tied to the azmari, whose role as performer and social commentator the ethnomusicologist Ashenafi Kebede described in a 1975 study in _The Musical Quarterly_. [The Musical Quarterly](https://doi.org/10.1093/mq/lxi.1.47)

## The Azmari and "Wax and Gold"

Azmari performances are as much about words as about music. Their verse draws on the Amharic poetic device known as _sem-enna-werq_, "wax and gold". In the sociologist Donald Levine's summary, the "wax" is the obvious meaning of a line and the "gold" is the hidden one. [University of Chicago Press: Donald N. Levine, Wax and Gold](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/W/bo19351153.html)

The music magazine PAM compares the azmari to West African griots as entertainers and social commentators. It says they have carried the krar through the Ethiopian highlands since at least the 15th century, and that they still play at weddings, in hotels and in the honey-wine bars known as _tej bet_. [PAM](https://pan-african-music.com/en/survey-of-african-harps/)

## The Krar: Five or Six Strings

The krar is a lyre with five or six strings, played in Ethiopia and Eritrea. Its decorated wooden body fans out to a crossbar (the yoke) to which the strings are bound. [PAM](https://pan-african-music.com/en/survey-of-african-harps/) It is tuned to a pentatonic (five-note) scale and can be plucked or strummed; strumming gives more volume and overtones. [PAM](https://pan-african-music.com/en/survey-of-african-harps/)

The krar has also changed with the times. Strings that were once tied with leather thongs or wooden pegs are now often fixed with the metal tuning heads used on electric guitars. Because a lyre is quiet, modern players often build pickups into their instruments so the krar can be amplified and played with a band, rather than only in the solo setting of the traditional azmari. [PAM](https://pan-african-music.com/en/survey-of-african-harps/)

## The Begena: A Lyre for Religious Song

The begena is a larger box lyre with a very different social role. The Horniman Museum's 18th-century begena from Tigray has a carved wooden resonator, a stitched leather soundboard decorated with geometric tooling, and six heavy gut strings, with fittings for four more. The museum's notes say that church texts associate the begena with the harp of King David, that it is played by plucking the long strings with fingers and thumbs, and that it usually accompanies songs on religious themes. [Horniman Museum: Box lyre, begena](https://www.horniman.ac.uk/object/M5.5.65/6) Oral tradition holds that Menelik I brought the instrument from Israel, where David had played it to soothe King Saul. [The Irish Times: Sunken Treasure: Alemu Aga's Ethiopiques Vol 11](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/music/sunken-treasure-alemu-aga-s-ethiopiques-vol-11-1.2775934) That story is a legend, not documented history.

The begena's recent history shows how closely music and politics can be tied. The begena master Alemu Aga, born near Addis Ababa in 1950, was first recorded in 1972 by the ethnomusicologist Cynthia Tse Kimberlin for a UNESCO collection. [Music in Africa: Alemu Aga](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/8894) After the Derg military government took power in 1974, its anti-religious policies included banning the begena from radio and closing the begena program at the Yared School of Music where he taught; he gave up his teaching post in 1980. [Music in Africa](https://www.musicinafrica.net/node/8894) His later album for the _Éthiopiques_ series pairs the ten-string begena with his voice. [The Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/music/sunken-treasure-alemu-aga-s-ethiopiques-vol-11-1.2775934)

## The Washint

The washint is an end-blown flute. The Horniman's example is a length of bamboo, open at both ends, with two internal nodes opened up and four small, round finger holes along the front. Like other washint, it probably plays a pentatonic scale. [Horniman Museum: Washint](https://www.horniman.ac.uk/object/M19.4.66/3)

## The Kebero and Church Chant

The church's music is attributed to Saint Yared, a scholar who lived in Aksum in the 6th century and to whom the hymnary (_Deggua_) is credited, though hymns by later composers were added over the centuries. [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/church/music.html) The chant uses three modes: _Ge'ez_ for ordinary days, _Ezel_ for fast days and Lent, and _Araray_ for the principal feasts. [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/church/music.html)

Chanting priests are accompanied by three instruments: the _tsenatsil_, a sistrum (a metal rattle); the kebero, a large drum; and a hand-bell. [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/church/music.html) The same page describes the priests swaying to the measured rhythm of the drums as they chant. [Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church](https://www.ethiopianorthodox.org/english/church/music.html)

The modes these instruments are tuned to became the foundation of a modern genre; see [Ethio-jazz and Mulatu Astatke](/en/music/ethio-jazz-mulatu-astatke). The church music of the begena and kebero is heard most publicly at the feasts described in [Ethiopia's religious festivals](/en/culture/ethiopian-religious-festivals).
