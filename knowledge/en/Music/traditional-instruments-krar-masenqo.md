# Traditional Ethiopian Instruments: Masenqo, Krar, and Washint

The musical traditions of Ethiopia are grounded in an indigenous acoustic ecosystem that dates back over a millennium, from the 6th-century sacred chants of Saint Yared to the vibrant folk poetry of highland minstrels. [Shelemay: Sounding the Center](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt14jxtph) Across village celebrations, sacred church liturgies, and lively _azmari bet_ music houses, traditional instruments carry the emotional and narrative heartbeat of Ethiopian society. [Kimberlin: The Masinqo and the Nature of the Azmari](https://www.jstor.org/stable/851221)

## The Masenqo: The Voice of the Azmari

The **Masenqo** is a single-stringed bowed lute crafted from wood and parchment. Its resonant body consists of four diamond-shaped wooden frames covered tightly in stretched raw oxhide or goatskin. Both the single playing string and the string of the short, curved wooden bow (_dägga_) are made from braided coarse horsehair. [Kimberlin](https://www.jstor.org/stable/851221)

The masenqo is the inseparable companion of the **Azmari**—the hereditary wandering bards and musical chroniclers of the Ethiopian highlands. As the Azmari plays, sliding fingers along the hair string to produce microtonal glissandos and vocal-like cries, they improvise spontaneous oral poetry:

- Praising hosts, roasting local dignitaries, or commenting on current political developments.
- Employing the ancient Amharic poetic convention of **Sem-enna-Worq** ("Wax and Gold"), where an obvious surface meaning (_the wax_) conceals an incisive, double-edged hidden satire (_the gold_).

## The Krar: Ancient Highland Bowl-Lyre

The **Krar** is a five- or six-stringed chordophone belonging to the ancient bowl-lyre family, closely related structurally to the lyres of ancient Mesopotamia and Greece. In Ethiopian spiritual lore, it stands in complementary contrast to the larger ten-stringed _begena_ (harp of David): while the begena is reserved exclusively for somber religious meditation and fasting hymns, the krar is the spirited vessel of secular festivities, love songs, and weddings. [Shelemay](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt14jxtph)

Musicians employ two distinct performance techniques on the krar:

1. **Plucking (_qinat_)**: Finger-picking individual strings to weave intricate, polyphonic melodies.
2. **Strumming (_təfər_)**: Rapidly sweeping a plectrum across all strings while using the left fingers to mute and damp unwanted strings.

In contemporary urban music, the traditional wooden krar has evolved into the **electric krar**, fitted with magnetic guitar pickups and wah-wah pedals to drive modern Ethiopian rock, jazz, and dance rhythms.

## The Washint and Kebero

- **Washint**: An end-blown oblique flute crafted from highland mountain bamboo or river reed, featuring four finger holes. Traditionally played by solitary highland shepherds herding cattle across vast plateaus, the washint is renowned for its melancholic, breathy timbre and intricate ornamentation that mirrors birdsong.
- **Kebero**: A conical, two-headed wooden hand drum wrapped in cowhide. Large versions are used exclusively in Ethiopian Orthodox Christian liturgies (_Mahlet_) to mark the rhythmic cycles of Saint Yared's sacred chants, while smaller variants propel rhythmic shoulder-shaking dances (_eskista_) during communal celebrations.

These acoustic instruments continue to anchor Ethiopian musical identity, serving as the raw organic foundation from which modern Ethio-Jazz, hip-hop, and pop draw their cultural strength.
