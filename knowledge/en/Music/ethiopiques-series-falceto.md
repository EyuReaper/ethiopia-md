# The Ethiopiques Series and Francis Falceto: Reissuing the Golden Years

## 30-second overview

Éthiopiques is a series of compact discs of Ethiopian music produced by the Paris label Buda Musique and curated by Francis Falceto, a French producer. It began in 1997, and many early volumes compile recordings from the 1960s and 1970s that came from Ethiopian labels such as Amha Records, Kaifa and Philips-Ethiopia, while some later volumes hold new recordings. [Wikipedia: Éthiopiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89thiopiques) The series is the main reason that the modern music of Addis Ababa's "golden years" is known abroad. It is also an outsider's project, shaped by one curator's account, so this article leans on his own interviews and on journalists, and flags where counts differ. For the musician most associated with it, see [Ethio-jazz and Mulatu Astatke](/en/music/ethio-jazz-mulatu-astatke).

## How it began

In a 2006 Afropop Worldwide interview, Falceto said he discovered Ethiopian music in April 1984 when a friend played him an LP by the singer Mahmoud Ahmed, bought in Addis Ababa. He made cassettes, sent them to music journalists who did not know the music either, and traveled to Ethiopia the following month. The first release abroad of modern Ethiopian music, he said, was a 1986 LP of Mahmoud Ahmed on the Crammed Discs label in Brussels. Around 1990 he recorded two CDs in Paris with Alèmayèhu Eshèté and Netsanet Mèllèssè, and in 1994 he released an oldies collection, _Ethiopian Groove_. The Éthiopiques CD series began in 1997. [Eyre, Afropop Worldwide 2006](https://old.afropop.org/articles/francis-falceto-ethiopia-empire-and-revolution) Robin Denselow, in the Mail & Guardian, similarly writes that Falceto "has been rediscovering and reissuing that music since 1986," tracking down original reel-to-reel masters in work he calls "often painstaking." [Denselow, Mail & Guardian 2007](https://mg.co.za/article/2007-10-26-ethiopia-france-the-world)

Buda Musique itself is a French label founded in 1987 by Gilles Fruchaux and Dominique Buscall, and is best known for the Éthiopiques series. [Wikipedia: Buda Musique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buda_Musique)

## The golden years, in Falceto's telling

Falceto places the golden years of modern Ethiopian music between 1955 and 1974. The Haile Selassie Theatre opened in 1955, and he traces modern music to military bands, citing a gift of 40 brass instruments from a Russian envoy to Menelik II and the 40 Armenian orphans Ras Tafari brought to Addis Ababa in 1924, who became the royal band. [Afropop Worldwide](https://old.afropop.org/articles/francis-falceto-ethiopia-empire-and-revolution) These are his historical summaries and are not independently checked here. He says that in the late 1960s and early 1970s young musicians, not the radio audience, pushed the new sound, influenced by Peace Corps volunteers, a US military base in Asmara with its own radio, and travelers returning with records. Amha Eshèté, a music shop owner, became an independent producer, releasing Alèmayèhu Eshèté's first single in 1969; Falceto calls him "the patron saint of the modern Ethiopian musician." [Afropop Worldwide](https://old.afropop.org/articles/francis-falceto-ethiopia-empire-and-revolution)

The Mail & Guardian adds that Amha's vinyl records were recorded in Ethiopia but manufactured in India and make up much of the Éthiopiques collection. It also says that under Haile Selassie "all bands were controlled by Selassie's authorities and no private orchestras were allowed," so musicians worked in state bands such as the Imperial Bodyguard Band, and that the "golden age" ended with the 1974 overthrow of the emperor. [Denselow 2007](https://mg.co.za/article/2007-10-26-ethiopia-france-the-world) Falceto described the Derg that followed as an 18-year socialist military regime and said its restrictions meant that the Walias Band's guitarist and saxophonist could not leave Ethiopia, so he recorded with French musicians in Paris instead. [Afropop Worldwide](https://old.afropop.org/articles/francis-falceto-ethiopia-empire-and-revolution) Mulatu Astatke told Peter Culshaw that the Derg's curfew produced long overnight jam sessions, and that he avoided trouble by playing instrumental music. [Culshaw, The Arts Desk 2010](https://theartsdesk.com/new-music/ethiopiques-mulatu-astatke-and-story-ethiopian-jazz)

## What is in the series

The Wikipedia listing shows how wide the series is. Selected volumes include:

- Volume 4, _Ethio Jazz & Musique Instrumentale, 1969 to 1974_, by Mulatu Astatke (1998).
- Volume 2, _Tètchawèt! Urban Azmaris of the 90s_ (1998), and volume 27, a centennial of the first Ethiopian recordings featuring the azmari Tèssèma Eshèté in 1910 (2010); see [the azmari tradition](/en/music/azmari-tradition).
- Volumes 6, 7, 19 and 26 devoted to Mahmoud Ahmed.
- Volume 9 (Alemayehu Eshete), volume 14 (_Negus of Ethiopian Sax_, Getatchew Mekurya), volume 16 (_The Lady with the Krar_, Asnaketch Worku) and volume 17 (Tilahun Gessesse).
- Volume 11, _The Harp of King David_ (Alemu Aga), and volume 21, _Ethiopia Song_ by Emahoy Tsegué-Maryam Guèbrou (2006).
- Volume 28 (_Great Oromo Music_, Ali Mohammed Birra) and volume 30 (_Mistakes on Purpose_, Girma Bèyènè and Akalé Wubé, 2017), which contains present-day music rather than reissues.

[Wikipedia: Éthiopiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89thiopiques) The Arts Desk adds that volume 21 draws on two 1963 LPs. [Culshaw 2010](https://theartsdesk.com/new-music/ethiopiques-mulatu-astatke-and-story-ethiopian-jazz) Falceto told Afropop that Getatchew Mekurya developed a saxophone style inspired by war-song vocals around 1953 to 1954, and that Asnaqètch Wèrqu, a krar player and actress, was a pioneer for women on stage. [Afropop Worldwide](https://old.afropop.org/articles/francis-falceto-ethiopia-empire-and-revolution) For the instruments behind some of these recordings, see [traditional instruments: krar and masenqo](/en/music/traditional-instruments-krar-masenqo).

## How many volumes?

Counts depend on the date and the source. The 2006 Afropop interview called it a "21-volume (and growing)" series; the Mail & Guardian in October 2007 said it would reach volume 23 within weeks; The Arts Desk in 2010 said it numbered 27; and Wikipedia says both that there were 30 releases as of 2017 and that 32 volumes have been released, listing two dated 2026. [Afropop Worldwide](https://old.afropop.org/articles/francis-falceto-ethiopia-empire-and-revolution); [Denselow 2007](https://mg.co.za/article/2007-10-26-ethiopia-france-the-world); [Culshaw 2010](https://theartsdesk.com/new-music/ethiopiques-mulatu-astatke-and-story-ethiopian-jazz); [Wikipedia: Éthiopiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89thiopiques) The Wikipedia list is internally inconsistent and should be checked against the label before the figure is cited.

## Broken Flowers and the wider audience

Wikipedia says volume 4 brought Mulatu Astatke's music to an international audience, and that Jim Jarmusch's film _Broken Flowers_ (2005) featured his music, which helped build his Western audience. [Wikipedia: Mulatu Astatke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulatu_Astatke) The Arts Desk likewise says the film used his music and helped broaden his audience. [Culshaw 2010](https://theartsdesk.com/new-music/ethiopiques-mulatu-astatke-and-story-ethiopian-jazz) Sources disagree on how many of his tracks the film used, so this article gives no number. The Wikipedia article also says that Astatke and the Massachusetts band Either/Orchestra met in Addis Ababa in 2004, began performing together in 2006, and made the 2010 album _Mulatu Steps Ahead_. [Wikipedia: Mulatu Astatke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulatu_Astatke)

## A note on perspective

Falceto's accounts are the backbone of the English-language story, but the series presents Ethiopian music through a French curator's selection. Falceto himself, in the Arts Desk interview, said that the pop and jazz scene in Addis had become less interesting in his view, with often only a singer and a synthesizer in clubs, a judgment that other listeners may not share. [Culshaw 2010](https://theartsdesk.com/new-music/ethiopiques-mulatu-astatke-and-story-ethiopian-jazz) Ethiopian musicians and producers, including Amha Eshèté, are the creators of the recordings the series preserves.

## Closing

Éthiopiques is both an archive and an introduction. It rescued recordings that might have been lost, and it let listeners abroad hear a music that Ethiopians had been making and selling for decades.
