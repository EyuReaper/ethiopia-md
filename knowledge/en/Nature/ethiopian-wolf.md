# The Ethiopian Wolf: Africa's Rarest Carnivore of the Afroalpine Highlands

**In 30 seconds.** The Ethiopian wolf (ቀይ ቀበሮ, _ky kebero_) is a slender, red-and-white canid that hunts rodents on the treeless Afroalpine grasslands of Ethiopia's high mountains. Conservation scientists call it the rarest canid in the world and Africa's most endangered carnivore. Its latest population estimate is around 450 animals in six isolated mountain areas, with the Bale Mountains holding more than half. Rabies and shrinking habitat are the main threats, and a long-running vaccination programme has become a model for wildlife disease management.

**A wolf, not a fox.** The animal looks fox-like, and its Amharic name ቀይ ቀበሮ contains the word for fox (_kebero_), but it is a wolf: its scientific name, _Canis simensis_, places it in _Canis_, the genus of the grey wolf (_Canis lupus_). [Wikipedia: Ethiopian wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_wolf)

## A highland specialist

The Wildlife Conservation Research Unit (WildCRU) at the University of Oxford describes the Ethiopian wolf (_Canis simensis_), known locally as _ky kebero_ or _jedala farda_, as a species found only in the Afroalpine highlands of Ethiopia, above about 3,000 m. It has long limbs and an elongated muzzle and is a specialised hunter of small highland mammals. Although each wolf hunts alone, wolves live in packs of 3 to 17 at high densities, up to 2 per km², defending exclusive territories around a dominant breeding pair, with cooperative breeding. [WildCRU: Ethiopian wolves](https://www.wildcru.org/?p=1199)

That specialisation shows in the diet. A 2018 field study of wolves at Guassa found that the Ethiopian wolf's diet was dominated by rodents and that the animals used mostly intact habitat. [Gutema et al., Royal Society Open Science (2018): Competition between sympatric wolf taxa](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5990763)

## How many are left

Counts have varied with method and year, so ranges are more honest than a single number.

- WildCRU says fewer than 500 adults remain in six isolated mountain ranges, with the largest population in the Bale Mountains accounting for over half; other populations are critically small, some with fewer than 25 adults. It cites a new 2024 estimate of about 450 wolves in 99 packs across six isolated populations, and notes that wolves have gone extinct in recent decades at Mount Guna and Gosh Meda. [WildCRU](https://www.wildcru.org/?p=1199)
- The Ethiopian Wolf Conservation Programme (EWCP) lists 6 populations, 122 packs and an estimated 453 wolves on its homepage, with an Endangered status. [EWCP](https://www.ethiopianwolf.org/) The difference in pack counts between the two sources is a reminder that definitions and survey years differ.
- The species is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List, according to both of the sources above. The Red List page itself blocked automated access when we tried it, so check it directly for the current assessment text and date.

The Bale Mountains are the species' stronghold. A 1992 _Oryx_ paper already described the Bale population as the largest and probably the only genetically viable one, and the species' range has since been reduced to six locations. [Gottelli and Sillero-Zubiri, Oryx (1992): The Ethiopian wolf, an endangered endemic canid](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0030605300023735) For the park itself, see [the Bale Mountains](/en/nature/bale-mountains-ecosystem).

## Threats

WildCRU lists the major threats as habitat loss and fragmentation due to agriculture and overgrazing, disease outbreaks (particularly rabies and canine distemper), road kills, and persecution, which it describes as relatively rare. Inbreeding and genetic isolation pose a potential risk, though no significant loss of fitness has been observed yet. [WildCRU](https://www.wildcru.org/?p=1199) Expanding agriculture and grazing bring wolves into closer contact with domestic dogs, which the University of Oxford's case study on this research identifies as the main reservoir of rabies. [UK Research Excellence Framework case study: A novel vaccination strategy to safeguard the Ethiopian wolf from extinction](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=23625)

Competition is a newer concern. At Guassa, researchers documented competition between the Ethiopian wolf and the African wolf (_Canis lupaster_, once regarded as the golden jackal), which used areas disturbed by people and livestock. [Gutema et al. (2018)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5990763)

## Rabies and the vaccination response

Rabies outbreaks have caused the sharpest losses. The REF case study reports that outbreaks occur every five to ten years with mortality of up to 77% in affected populations, and that in the early 1990s 77% of the largest wolf population, in Bale, died or disappeared over about four months, with dog-associated rabies the suspected cause. [REF case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=23625)

A major outbreak in 2003 to 2004 gave the team a chance to test a new approach. A paper in _Emerging Infectious Diseases_ reports that, before August 2003, the Web Valley in Bale held an estimated 95 wolves, and describes how parenteral (injected) vaccination was used to manage the outbreak. [Randall et al., Emerging Infectious Diseases (2004): Rabies in Endangered Ethiopian Wolves](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3323365) According to the REF case study, more than three-quarters (76%) of wolves in the Web Valley died within less than six months, while just under 40% of surviving wolves in neighbouring packs were vaccinated in a cordon sanitaire, which halted the spread along the narrow valley corridors the wolves use. Modelling had suggested that vaccinating as few as 20 to 40% of wolves might protect against the largest epizootics. [REF case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=23625)

The EWCP now frames its work in "One Health" terms, managing disease with benefits for people, wildlife and livestock, as one of five core projects across the Ethiopian Highlands. [EWCP](https://www.ethiopianwolf.org/)

## Why the Afroalpine matters

Because wolves sit near the top of a specialised food chain, protecting them means protecting the whole Afroalpine system of grasslands, rodents and giant lobelias. The EWCP states that by protecting the wolves it safeguards other Afroalpine endemics. [EWCP](https://www.ethiopianwolf.org/) A Wikipedia overview adds that the wolf's current range is limited to isolated mountains at altitudes of about 3,000 to 4,500 m and that earlier in its evolutionary history the species had a more continuous distribution; that history is a summary of the literature rather than a settled fact. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_wolf)

## Closing

The Ethiopian wolf is a measure of how a whole mountain ecosystem is faring. With only a few hundred animals left, a single outbreak can matter, which is why monitoring, vaccination and habitat protection continue side by side.
