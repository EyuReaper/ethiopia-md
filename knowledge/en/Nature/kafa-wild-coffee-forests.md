# The Wild Coffee Forests of Kafa: Where Arabica Grows Native

**In 30 seconds.** Arabica coffee (_Coffea arabica_), the species behind most of the world's coffee, is native to the highland forests of south-western Ethiopia. In Kafa, around the town of Bonga, a UNESCO biosphere reserve protects the cloud forest in which wild coffee grows under the tree canopy. Scientists value these forests as a reservoir of genetic diversity for breeding. Climate models suggest that suitable conditions for wild Arabica could shrink sharply this century, which is why the forest's conservation has attracted international attention.

## Where and what

The Kafa Biosphere Reserve lies in the Kafa Zone, about 460 km south-west of Addis Ababa, with Bonga as the nearest city. [Wikipedia: Kafa Biosphere Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafa_Biosphere_Reserve) It was designated by UNESCO in 2010, and the United Nations office in Ethiopia describes it as Ethiopia's first UNESCO-protected biosphere reserve. [UN Ethiopia: From Ethiopia's biosphere reserve](https://ethiopia.un.org/en/node/306172) The German conservation group NABU, which helped prepare the application, says it spans 760,000 hectares and includes cloud forests, wetlands, rivers and tropical rainforests. [NABU: The Kafa Biosphere Reserve in Ethiopia](https://en.nabu.de/projects/ethiopia/kafa/biodiversity) Wikipedia, by contrast, lists an area of 2,193 km² (about 219,000 hectares). [Wikipedia: Kafa Biosphere Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafa_Biosphere_Reserve) The two figures are far apart. The difference may reflect which zones are counted, but we could not open UNESCO's own record to confirm it, so treat the area as uncertain.

NABU reports that systematic forest surveys in 2014 found around 358,000 hectares of cloud forest and 17,000 hectares of bamboo forest, with more than 5,000 hectares of wetlands under protection, and that almost half of the forest area had been lost before the area was recognised. [NABU](https://en.nabu.de/projects/ethiopia/kafa/biodiversity) The same page says two biodiversity surveys in 2014 and 2019 documented more than 50 species new to science and recorded around 300 bird species, though these are NABU's own project figures rather than a peer-reviewed count.

## Why it matters for coffee

Scientific literature supports the claim that this region is the centre of Arabica's native range. A 2012 paper in _PLOS ONE_ states that the largest and most diverse populations of indigenous (wild) Arabica occur in the highlands of south-western Ethiopia, with satellite populations in south-eastern South Sudan and northern Kenya at altitudes of roughly 950 to 1,950 m. [Davis et al., PLOS ONE (2012): The Impact of Climate Change on Indigenous Arabica Coffee](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0047981) The paper also notes that coffee made up about a third of Ethiopia's export earnings at the time of writing, and that the wild gene pool had an estimated value to the coffee industry of 0.5 to 1.5 billion US dollars a year.

NABU describes Kafa as regarded as a global gene bank of more than 5,000 varieties and sub-varieties of Arabica and says UNESCO explicitly cited Kafa's role as the "Origin and Center of Arabica Coffee". [NABU](https://en.nabu.de/projects/ethiopia/kafa/biodiversity) The count of 5,000 varieties is NABU's characterisation and is not taken from a primary genetic survey in the sources used here; read it as an indication of diversity, not a measured figure.

The World Bank similarly writes that Kafa holds thousands of genetic variants of coffee, "an important seed bank of wild Arabica that faces extinction as a result of climate change". It adds that about 15 million people in Ethiopia depend on coffee for their livelihoods, and that Ethiopia is Africa's biggest coffee producer and the world's fifth-largest exporter. [World Bank (2020): Why Biodiversity Matters to Your Daily Cup of Coffee](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/feature/2020/06/04/why-biodiversity-matters-to-your-daily-cup-of-coffee) For the human side of coffee, see [Ethiopian coffee culture](/en/culture/ethiopian-coffee-culture).

## Climate threat: the numbers

Two peer-reviewed modelling studies give a sense of the risk, with large ranges that reflect uncertainty about future emissions and how species can move.

- **Wild Arabica.** Davis and colleagues modelled bioclimatic suitability under three emissions scenarios to 2080. In a locality analysis, the most favourable outcome was a reduction of about 65% in the number of suitable localities, and the worst an almost 100% reduction. In an area analysis, the most favourable outcome was a 38% reduction in suitable space and the least favourable about 90%. [Davis et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0047981)
- **Coffee farming.** Moat and colleagues projected that 39 to 59% of Ethiopia's current coffee-growing area could become unsuitable under various climate-change scenarios if nothing else changes. Relocating coffee areas while conserving or re-establishing forest could, they estimate, give at least a fourfold (over 400%) increase in suitable area. [Moat et al., Nature Plants (2017): Resilience potential of the Ethiopian coffee sector under climate change](https://doi.org/10.1038/nplants.2017.81)

These are projections rather than observations, and both papers used climate models that have since been updated.

## People and the forest

Wild coffee is also a livelihood. NABU reports that cooperatives in the reserve, mainly for coffee, honey and spices, include around 35 cooperatives with some 20,000 members, and that the Kafa Forest Coffee Union markets products internationally. [NABU](https://en.nabu.de/projects/ethiopia/kafa/biodiversity) A United Nations feature on a specialty coffee producer in the reserve quotes her saying the local rule is simple: "don't cut the big trees", and that "if we lose our forests, we lose our coffee". [UN Ethiopia](https://ethiopia.un.org/en/node/306172) NABU also notes that agriculture employs about 80% of the region's roughly 700,000 inhabitants.

The conservation model is a biosphere reserve, which combines strict protection of selected core areas with sustainable use by local communities. [NABU](https://en.nabu.de/projects/ethiopia/kafa/biodiversity) Whether those arrangements can keep pace with forest clearance and a warming climate is one of the open questions.

## Closing

Kafa shows how a global commodity depends on a particular forest. The forest is also a home and an income for the people who live in it, and keeping both intact is the aim of the reserve.
