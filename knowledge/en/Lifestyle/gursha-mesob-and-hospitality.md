# Gursha, the Mesob and Ethiopian Hospitality at the Table

## 30-second overview

In Ethiopia a meal is usually shared. People eat from one platter, with the right hand, using injera to pick up the food. The host may feed a guest a mouthful by hand, an act called _gursha_ (ጉርሻ). The injera itself is often stored and served in a _mesob_ (መሶብ), a woven basket that has become a national symbol. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_cuisine); [Wikipedia: Mesob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesob) This article describes those customs, and is careful to separate documented practice from the generalizations that travel guides often repeat. Practice varies by region, religion and household, and what follows is a common pattern, not a rule.

## Eating from one platter

The Cultural Atlas, a guide for cross-cultural understanding, says that Ethiopian meals often use no utensils, that people scoop food with their fingers, usually with injera, and that guests share a platter rather than individual plates. It adds that one should never pass or eat food with the left hand alone. [Cultural Atlas: Ethiopian Culture, Etiquette](https://theculturalatlas.org/ethiopian-culture/ethiopian-culture-etiquette) Wikipedia likewise says Ethiopians usually eat with their right hands, using pieces of injera to pick up bites. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_cuisine)

In a 2012 KQED piece, restaurant owner Fetlework Tefferi describes the food as served family style on a large circle of injera that serves as tablecloth, plate and utensil, and calls eating "almost a sacred ritual." In her account no one leaves until everyone has finished and the tray is removed, and diners wash their hands before eating and eat only with the right hand, a rule children are taught early. [Mindess, KQED 2012](https://www.kqed.org/bayareabites/39132/eat-with-your-hands-for-a-) This is one restaurateur's description, not a survey. Wikipedia's article on injera adds that a typical shared meal is a stack of injera with dishes on top, and that two or three people may share one injera. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera) For the bread itself, see [injera and teff](/en/food/injera-and-teff).

## Gursha

_Gursha_ means a mouthful in Amharic. The anthropologist Hagar Salamon, in a 2020 article in Northeast African Studies, describes it as a practice in which a host hand-feeds a seated guest a bite of rich food. Her study gathers the many associations linked to this hospitable custom and treats gursha as a flexible setting where social, historical and religious meanings meet, a "dominant gesture" that combines secular and religious sentiment. [Salamon, Northeast African Studies 19(2), 2020](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/from-hand-to-mouth-reflections-on-the-multivocality-of-gursha-in-/) This article relies on the abstract. [DOI 10.14321/nortafristud.19.2.0001](https://doi.org/10.14321/nortafristud.19.2.0001)

The Cultural Atlas says guests may be hand-fed the best parts of the meal and that it is polite to smile and accept. [Cultural Atlas](https://theculturalatlas.org/ethiopian-culture/ethiopian-culture-etiquette) Tefferi calls it "a loving act, an endearment," used for children who are not eating enough or for guests, and jokes that feeding a man is "a macho thing," requiring a larger roll of injera. [Mindess, KQED 2012](https://www.kqed.org/bayareabites/39132/eat-with-your-hands-for-a-) The sources agree that gursha is an expression of regard. Beyond that, claims about who feeds whom, how large the mouthful should be or whether it can be refused vary and are mostly anecdotal, so this article does not state fixed rules.

## The mesob

Wikipedia describes the mesob as a Harari basket used to store injera, woven from locally grown grass and palm leaves, some partially dyed, and widely treated as a cultural symbol of Ethiopia. An early form was reported in Abyssinia in the 16th century by the Portuguese traveler Francisco Álvares; the most prominent mesob is of Harar origin, Harar mesobs are considered high quality, and in the mid-1970s Amhara patrons recruited Harari artisans to make a version blending Abyssinian and Harari designs that came to dominate production. [Wikipedia: Mesob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesob) For the town where the craft is strongest, see [Harar Jugol](/en/culture/harar-jugol-walled-city).

The Injera article says a picture of a mesob appears on the 10-birr banknote, that non-family participants carry mesobs full of injera at funerals to support the bereaved, and that Orthodox fast-day meals often serve injera in a mesob. [Wikipedia: Injera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injera); [Wikipedia: Mesob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesob) Wikipedia's cuisine article says a typical dish is traditionally consumed on the mesob basket. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_cuisine) Most of the claims about the mesob rest on Wikipedia, which cites standard reference works, so they are best checked against those works for scholarly use.

## Visiting and receiving

The Cultural Atlas lists customs around visits. Friends and family often visit without notice, though urban visits are more often pre-arranged. Visitors should expect to be offered a drink on arrival, usually coffee, and an invitation to eat; it is polite to decline at first and accept on the second or third offer, as hosts will likely repeat their invitation. Visitors should plan for a visit of at least an hour and remove their shoes before entering someone's home, a church or a mosque. [Cultural Atlas](https://theculturalatlas.org/ethiopian-culture/ethiopian-culture-etiquette)

At the table, the same source says hosts may not sit until their guests are seated, that the eldest or most esteemed guests are served first, that one waits for the blessing before eating, and that it is considered inappropriate to leave right after the meal. [Cultural Atlas](https://theculturalatlas.org/ethiopian-culture/ethiopian-culture-etiquette) Gifts are given and received with two hands or the right hand only and are not opened on receipt, and the guide advises against alcohol as a gift to Muslim or Protestant Ethiopians. [Cultural Atlas](https://theculturalatlas.org/ethiopian-culture/ethiopian-culture-etiquette) The Cultural Atlas is a general guide, and its advice reflects widespread urban customs more than every community.

## Coffee after the meal

The visit usually ends, or continues, with coffee. The Cultural Atlas notes that the woman of the household typically leads the ceremony, that coffee is served in three rounds called _abol_, _tonna_ and _baraka_ with the eldest poured first, and that it is inappropriate to leave after the first round. [Cultural Atlas](https://theculturalatlas.org/ethiopian-culture/ethiopian-culture-etiquette) The full ceremony is described in [Ethiopian coffee culture](/en/culture/ethiopian-coffee-culture). The same guide says the ceremony takes at least an hour.

## Closing

The shared platter, the hand-fed bite and the woven basket tell one story: in this tradition, food is not an individual possession but something a household offers outward. Guests who accept a gursha with a smile are following the most widely reported rule.
