# Running Culture: Bekoji, Entoto and the Ethiopian Highland Runner

## 30-second overview

Ethiopia's distance runners are known worldwide, but running at home is less a hobby than a way of life and, for many, a job. Two places anchor the story. Bekoji (በቆጂ), a highland town in the Arsi Zone of the Oromia Region, has produced a striking number of Olympic champions. Entoto, the forested mountain range above Addis Ababa, is where many athletes in the capital train. [Wikipedia: Bekoji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekoji); [Borenstein, Anthropology News 2023](https://www.anthropology-news.org/articles/running-in-the-dark/) No single cause explains the success; altitude, early running, coaching, diet and economic opportunity all appear in the evidence below, and sources disagree about their weight. For the sport's most famous founding figure, see [Abebe Bikila and the marathon legacy](/en/people/abebe-bikila-marathon-legacy).

## Bekoji: a small town with a long list

Bekoji lies in the Arsi Zone, about 56 km south of Asella, at an altitude Wikipedia gives as about 2,810 m. Population figures vary: Wikipedia cites an estimate of 16,730 for 2005, TIME described a farming and herding community of about 33,000 in 2008, and the Tempo Journal says about 50,000 today. [Wikipedia: Bekoji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekoji); [Larmer, TIME 2008](https://content.time.com/time/specials/2007/article/0,28804,1815747_1815707_1815673,00.html); [Balcha, Tempo Journal](https://tempojournal.com/article/bokoji-the-ethiopian-town-that-raised-champions) Wikipedia lists Derartu Tulu, Fatuma Roba, Tiki Gelana, Mestawet Tufa, the Bekele brothers Kenenisa and Tariku, and the Dibaba sisters Ejegayehu, Tirunesh and Genzebe as natives. [Wikipedia: Bekoji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekoji) Athletics Weekly records that Tulu won the Olympic 10,000 m in 1992 and 2000, that Roba was the first African woman to win an Olympic marathon, in Atlanta in 1996, and that Tirunesh Dibaba won the 5,000 m and 10,000 m at the 2008 Beijing Olympics. [Middleton, Athletics Weekly 2016](https://athleticsweekly.com/?p=40485) The Tempo Journal adds, as the author's own claim, that Bokoji athletes took three of Ethiopia's four long-distance track golds at the 2008 Games. [Tempo Journal](https://tempojournal.com/article/bokoji-the-ethiopian-town-that-raised-champions) A 2012 documentary, _Town of Runners_, follows young athletes from the town. [Wikipedia: Bekoji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekoji)

## The coach and the school

Accounts of Bekoji repeatedly credit a teacher. TIME, in 2008, wrote that several top runners, including Tirunesh Dibaba and Kenenisa Bekele, attended Bekoji Elementary School and had the same childhood coach, whom it names Santayehu Eshetu. Other sources spell his name Sentayehu. TIME describes a former footballer known for punishing workouts, whose sessions were first planned for about 25 athletes and by then drew well over 100. [Larmer, TIME 2008](https://content.time.com/time/specials/2007/article/0,28804,1815747_1815707_1815673,00.html) Athletics Weekly says he spotted Derartu Tulu as a schoolgirl in his class, describes him as largely self-taught with detailed records on every athlete, and quotes the author's opinion that he is one of the greatest coaches ever. [Athletics Weekly](https://athleticsweekly.com/?p=40485) The Tempo Journal says he taught at the local primary school for decades and formalized the town's training, while noting that other teachers also contributed, and quotes him saying discipline matters more than talent alone. [Tempo Journal](https://tempojournal.com/article/bokoji-the-ethiopian-town-that-raised-champions) These are journalists' accounts rather than controlled studies, but they agree on the role of a structured, school-based start.

## Facilities: modest by any standard

Visitors are struck by how little the town has. Athletics Weekly describes a stony, bumpy track with grass reaching lane three in places, and no gym, physiotherapist, nutritionist or sports psychologist, and argues the success owes more to hard work, high standards and self-belief than to facilities. [Athletics Weekly](https://athleticsweekly.com/?p=40485) The Tempo Journal calls the stadium, begun about 25 years earlier, a muddy field with a patched fence, and describes children who run long distances out of necessity, herding cattle or reaching school. [Tempo Journal](https://tempojournal.com/article/bokoji-the-ethiopian-town-that-raised-champions)

## Is it altitude, diet or genes?

Researchers have looked for an explanation. A 2011 paper in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition notes that successful Ethiopian distance runners largely come from the Arsi region, a high-altitude area with about 5 percent of the national population, which supplied 14 of the 23 distance runners on Ethiopia's 2008 Olympic team. [Beis et al., JISSN 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3117768) The same study measured the diet of 10 elite runners (8 men, 2 women) and found a high-carbohydrate pattern: 9.7 g per kg of body mass per day, about 64 percent of energy, which meets endurance-athlete recommendations, with protein at 1.8 g per kg and fat at 23.3 percent of energy. Drinks supplied about 1,751 mL a day, mostly water, and the athletes drank nothing before or during training, so fluid intake fell short of recommendations. [Beis et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3117768) With only 10 participants, this describes one group, not all runners.

Journalists weigh the factors differently. TIME presents altitude as one factor alongside genetics and early running, quotes Haile Gebrselassie crediting early running, and says daily life itself becomes "a kind of high-altitude training." [TIME 2008](https://content.time.com/time/specials/2007/article/0,28804,1815747_1815707_1815673,00.html) Athletics Weekly notes that some argue altitude is key but says it does not explain why Bekoji has produced so many champions when larger towns in similar conditions have not. [Athletics Weekly](https://athleticsweekly.com/?p=40485) Altitude figures themselves vary, from about 2,800 to 3,000 m in the Tempo Journal to nearly 10,000 feet in TIME.

## Running as work

Economics are part of the explanation. TIME says Bekoji is not desperately poor by Ethiopian standards, but families in outlying villages often live hand to mouth, and running offers one of the few ways out. [TIME 2008](https://content.time.com/time/specials/2007/article/0,28804,1815747_1815707_1815673,00.html) The anthropologist Hannah Borenstein, writing in Anthropology News in 2023 about one elite management group in Addis Ababa, found athletes calling non-athletes "civil" workers and insisting that running "is her work." [Borenstein 2023](https://www.anthropology-news.org/articles/running-in-the-dark/) She cautions that her essay concerns a single group and should not be generalized.

## A day in Addis Ababa: before dawn

Borenstein describes waking well before daybreak as part of the athletes' routine; her own day began at 4:23 a.m. Training groups meet at a central hub and travel by minibus to training sites, and commutes can be long, with a 30-minute outbound trip taking over two hours on the return. [Borenstein 2023](https://www.anthropology-news.org/articles/running-in-the-dark/) One athlete told her that "running after [11:00 a.m.] is not productive," an opinion from a single 2014 interview. Many runners live in the Kotobe neighborhood, whose forest "stretches back to the Entoto Mountains"; athletes without cars use it for afternoon runs and light sessions, and hill and endurance sessions are held there. She notes that the forest has shrunk as development projects eroded its borders. [Borenstein 2023](https://www.anthropology-news.org/articles/running-in-the-dark/)

## Closing

The best-sourced account of Ethiopian running is modest: a few teachers, many early mornings, thin air, thin facilities, and a society in which running is among the more reachable ways to build a life.
