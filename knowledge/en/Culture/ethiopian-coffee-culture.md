# Ethiopian Coffee Culture and the Coffee Ceremony

For Ethiopians, coffee is not merely a drink but a daily ritual through which hospitality, neighborliness and family bonds are expressed. The Arabica variety (Coffea arabica) grows wild in the mountain forests of southern Ethiopia, and today the country is Africa's largest exporter of Arabica coffee. [Kew: Arabica coffee](https://www.kew.org/plants/arabica-coffee) The coffee ceremony, performed at home by women, unfolds over three rounds of brewing; it is a source of shared conversation, support and reconciliation.

## Coffee's Birthplace, Its Legend, and the Meaning of Its Name

Wild Coffea arabica is believed to have originated in the Ethiopian highlands, most often associated with the Kaffa region. [Britannica: History of coffee](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-coffee) UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere programme reports that the forests of Kaffa Zone, the genetic home and center of diversity for wild Arabica, contain some 5,000 varieties of wild coffee. [UNESCO MAB: Kafa Biosphere Reserve](https://www.unesco.org/en/mab/kafa) Several origin legends surround coffee's discovery, but the most widely known tale belongs to a goat herder named Kaldi: as the story goes, he noticed his goats behaving unusually energetically after eating berries from a coffee branch, tried the fruit himself out of curiosity, and was delighted. [Britannica: History of coffee](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-coffee) This legend is usually dated to around the 9th century CE, but researchers present it only as "one among many legends" — it is not verified history. Coffee plants are also said to have been taken across the Red Sea to southern Arabia (Yemen) around the 15th century CE, where they were cultivated — the beginning of coffee's spread into a global culture. [Britannica: Where did coffee originate?](https://www.britannica.com/question/Where-did-coffee-originate) Scholars also debate the etymology of the English word "coffee": word-history dictionaries trace it from Arabic qahwah via Ottoman Turkish and Dutch, while others link it to Kaffa, the region where the plant was named. In Kaffa, coffee is called būno, which is also said to have entered Arabic as bunn. [Etymonline: Coffee](https://www.etymonline.com/word/coffee)

## The Coffee Ceremony, Step by Step

By custom, the woman of the household performs the ceremony; it is regarded as a role of honor. [Wikipedia: Coffee ceremony of Ethiopia and Eritrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_ceremony_of_Ethiopia_and_Eritrea) Before the ceremony begins, freshly cut grass and flowers are strewn across the floor to welcome guests, and in both cities and villages the aroma of roasting coffee mingles with incense throughout the home. [UNESCO: Ethiopia, the home of coffee](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ethiopia-home-coffee) The green (unroasted) beans are washed before guests, roasted over coals in a flat pan, and when they come out smoking, the pan is carried around so guests can take in the fragrance, waving the smoke toward their noses. [UNESCO: Ethiopia, the home of coffee](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ethiopia-home-coffee) The roasted beans are then ground in a wooden mortar called a mukecha using a pestle known as a zenzena. [Wikipedia: Coffee ceremony of Ethiopia and Eritrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_ceremony_of_Ethiopia_and_Eritrea) The ground coffee goes into the jebena, a wide-bellied clay pot with a long neck and spout, where it brews over the coals; the hostess pours the finished coffee from a height of about thirty centimeters in one unbroken stream into small handleless cups called sini. In some areas a sprig of kosoret dipped into each cup adds aroma; sugar is the usual addition, though a pinch of salt may be used in rural areas where sugar is scarce. [UNESCO: Ethiopia, the home of coffee](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ethiopia-home-coffee)

## Abol, Tona, Baraka

What distinguishes the ceremony is that the grounds are brewed three times, and each round has its own name: in Amharic the rounds are called abol, tona and baraka. [Wikipedia: Coffee ceremony of Ethiopia and Eritrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_ceremony_of_Ethiopia_and_Eritrea) UNESCO notes that these words derive from Arabic and mean, in order, "first," "second" and "blessing," pointing to the ceremony's religious origins. [UNESCO: Ethiopia, the home of coffee](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ethiopia-home-coffee) Abol is the strong first round; tona is brewed again from the same grounds; and the final, weakest round, baraka, holds the highest place in the ritual — as its name suggests, the last cup is believed to bring blessing. Drinking all three rounds is counted as a sign of respect, while leaving before the third is widely considered rude. [The Guardian: 'It's rude not to offer three cups'](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2024/apr/23/its-rude-not-to-offer-three-cups-the-lengthy-beloved-coffee-rituals-binding-ethiopians-together) The full ceremony takes roughly an hour from raw bean to final cup, and neighbors take turns hosting so everyone shares the burden of the ritual. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2024/apr/23/its-rude-not-to-offer-three-cups-the-lengthy-beloved-coffee-rituals-binding-ethiopians-together) Incense burns alongside the brew — complementing the aroma of the coffee and, many believe, warding off evil spirits. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2024/apr/23/its-rude-not-to-offer-three-cups-the-lengthy-beloved-coffee-rituals-binding-ethiopians-together) Snacks such as popcorn, roasted grain (kolo), peanuts or himbasha bread accompany the coffee. [Wikipedia: Coffee ceremony of Ethiopia and Eritrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_ceremony_of_Ethiopia_and_Eritrea)

## A Pillar of Social Life

In Amharic, the phrase "buna tetu" literally means "drink coffee," but what it really conveys is "sit down, let's meet and talk" — clear evidence of coffee's place in Ethiopian social life. The ceremony is not reserved for family members alone: elders arrive carrying their own sugar and spices, young women practice welcoming guests with respect, and the lady of the house displays her skill — a genuine community gathering. [UNESCO: Ethiopia, the home of coffee](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ethiopia-home-coffee) The ceremony carries deep historical weight: until the late nineteenth century coffee drinking was largely confined to Muslims, but as the Christian kingdom expanded into the lowlands and absorbed Muslim communities, the custom spread across ethnic lines until it embraced all Ethiopians. [UNESCO: Ethiopia, the home of coffee](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ethiopia-home-coffee) The ceremony's roots lie in Sufi mystical tradition, where coffee was prepared repeatedly during devotional practices; vessels used for brewing came to be seen as carriers of baraka — spiritual blessing — and local sayings woven around coffee reinforce communal identity and mutual belonging. [UNESCO: Ethiopia, the home of coffee](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ethiopia-home-coffee)

## From Culture to Economy

Coffee culture is also a giant pillar of the Ethiopian economy. Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew reports that Ethiopia's coffee exports earn more than one billion US dollars a year on average. [Kew: Arabica coffee](https://www.kew.org/plants/arabica-coffee) Coffee cultivation accounts for roughly a quarter of the country's export earnings and provides livelihoods for an estimated fifteen million people. [Kew: Mainstreaming biodiversity in coffee-growing landscapes](https://www.kew.org/read-and-watch/mainstreaming-biodiversity-coffee-ethiopia) Moreover, much of Ethiopia's coffee is grown inside or beside natural forest systems — the Kaffa-Bonga, Yayu and Harenna coffee forests all appear on lists of major protected areas — making these landscapes places where coffee culture and nature conservation intertwine. [Kew: Mainstreaming biodiversity in coffee-growing landscapes](https://www.kew.org/read-and-watch/mainstreaming-biodiversity-coffee-ethiopia) Yet wild Arabica has been assessed as Endangered on the global Red List: climate change alone could reduce its native population by half or more by the year 2088. [Kew: Arabica coffee](https://www.kew.org/plants/arabica-coffee) Passing both the coffee culture and the forests on to the next generation has therefore become vital — not just economically but culturally, because Ethiopia is not only a country whose beans sell worldwide but one whose people still drink coffee daily within a living ceremony.

In Ethiopian culture, coffee stands alongside religious festivals and family gatherings as a second pillar of shared life. The ceremony weaves together festival celebration, women's roles and neighborhood relations, binding coffee to national identity.
