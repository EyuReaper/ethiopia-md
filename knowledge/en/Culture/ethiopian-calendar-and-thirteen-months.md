# The Ethiopian Calendar: Thirteen Months and a Different Count of Years

## 30-second overview

Ethiopia's civil and church calendar has twelve months of exactly 30 days and a thirteenth month, Pagume (ጳጉሜ), of five days, or six in a leap year. [timeanddate.com: The Ethiopian Calendar](https://www.timeanddate.com/calendar/ethiopia-calendar.html) Its year number trails the Gregorian one by seven or eight years, and its new year, Enkutatash, falls in September. On 21 November 2026 in the Gregorian calendar, the Ethiopian date was 12 Hidar 2019. The calendar is not a folk curiosity: Wikipedia describes it as the official state civil calendar of Ethiopia, and it also governs religious observance in Ethiopian Orthodox churches. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar)

## How the year is built

The months are, in order: Meskerem (መስከረም), Tikimt (ጥቅምት), Hidar (ኅዳር), Tahsas (ታኅሣሥ), Tir (ጥር), Yekatit (የካቲት), Megabit (መጋቢት), Miazia (ሚያዝያ), Genbot (ግንቦት), Sene (ሰኔ), Hamle (ሐምሌ), Nehase (ነሐሴ) and Pagume. Each of the first twelve has 30 days, so a month is a tidy unit and the date arithmetic is easy. The leftover five days (365 minus 360) make up Pagume. [timeanddate.com](https://www.timeanddate.com/calendar/ethiopia-calendar.html)

A leap day is added every four years without exception, as in the Julian calendar, which gives Pagume its sixth day. [timeanddate.com](https://www.timeanddate.com/calendar/ethiopia-calendar.html) Because the rule has no century exceptions, the Ethiopian calendar slowly drifts from the Gregorian one, and Wikipedia notes that the date correspondences it gives are reliable only from March 1900 to February 2100. The four-year cycle is also tied to the four Evangelists: the years after a leap year are named for John, Matthew and Mark, and the leap year itself is the year of Luke. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar)

Each month starts on the same day as its Coptic counterpart, a sign of the shared Alexandrian ancestry. Meskerem 1 falls on 11 September, or on 12 September in the year before a Gregorian leap year. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar) Pagume therefore falls in late August and early September, just before the new year. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar)

## Why the years are "seven or eight years behind"

Sources agree on the size of the gap but sometimes simplify its cause. The accurate version is this. The Ethiopian year is eight years behind the Gregorian year from 1 January until about 10 or 11 September, and seven years behind from then until 31 December, because the new year starts in September rather than January. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar)

The difference in the starting point goes back to how scholars in Alexandria dated the Incarnation. Wikipedia reports that around AD 400 the Alexandrian monk Panodoros fixed an era counting from a creation date of 5493 BC, and that Bishop Anianos later shifted the starting point by about six months to favor the Annunciation; his calculation became the basis of the Incarnation Era used by the Ethiopian and Eritrean churches. In AD 525 Dionysius Exiguus, whose system the West adopted, placed the Annunciation nine years earlier than Anianos had. [Wikipedia: Ethiopian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar) The Ethiopian epoch corresponds to 29 August AD 8 in the Julian calendar. In other words, Ethiopia did not "lose" seven years; two Christian chronologies disagree about one event, and Ethiopia kept the older Alexandrian one.

A widely repeated BBC explainer, republished by MyJoyOnline, says only that the calendar "calculates the birth year of Jesus differently." [BBC News, via MyJoyOnline](https://www.myjoyonline.com/ethiopia-the-country-where-a-year-lasts-13-months/) Popular summaries sometimes say that Ethiopia places Jesus's birth "in 7 BC"; that is a shortcut, not what the era actually counts from. This article does not repeat the BBC piece's phrase "seven years and eight months behind" or its remark that the new year falls "at the start of spring," since neither matches the September date and the two-gap rule above.

## Enkutatash, the new year

The new year, Enkutatash (እንቁጣጣሽ), is said to mean "gift of jewels." According to Ethiopian tradition, the Queen of Sheba (Makeda) returned from her visit to King Solomon in Jerusalem on 11 September, and her followers celebrated by giving her jewels. [Wikipedia: Enkutatash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enkutatash) Families go to church in the morning and share a meal of injera and wat, often doro wat. Girls in new clothes gather daisies (_adey abeba_), give friends bouquets, and go door to door singing and playing drums; in cities, greeting cards often replace the bouquets. Large celebrations take place across the country, notably at Raguel Church on Mount Entoto. [Wikipedia: Enkutatash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enkutatash) For the other feasts that follow the same calendar, see [Ethiopian religious festivals](/en/culture/ethiopian-religious-festivals).

## The church's calculation: Bahire Hasab

Movable feasts such as Easter (Fasika) are not read off the civil calendar but computed with the Bahire Hasab, a traditional system used by the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Churches. Wikipedia describes it as a conventional mathematical system for organizing religious dates, not an astronomical model. The calculation multiplies the year's position in a 19-year lunar cycle (the _Wenber_) by eleven and takes the remainder after dividing by thirty to get the _Abekte_; the _Metqi_ is 30 minus the Abekte. The Nineveh fast always begins on a Monday, and Fasika falls 69 days after it. Between 1901 and 2099 Ethiopian Orthodox Easter falls between 4 April and 8 May in the Gregorian calendar. [Wikipedia: Bahire Hasab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahre_Hasab)

A 2008 paper in the East African Journal of Sciences by S. Tafesse, "The Mathematical Basis of the Calendar Used by the Ethiopian Orthodox Twahedo Church for Fasting Periods and Religious Holidays," shows how modern number theory (the division algorithm and the theory of congruence) reproduces these dates. [Tafesse, East African Journal of Sciences 2(1), 2008](https://doi.org/10.4314/eajsci.v2i1.40368) The DOI resolves to the journal's record on African Journals Online; this article relies only on the paper's abstract and keywords, not on its full text.

## Counting the hours

Daily time follows a similar logic. The BBC piece reports that the Ethiopian day is split into two 12-hour periods that start at 06:00, so midday and midnight are both "six o'clock." A meeting set for ten o'clock in Addis Ababa could therefore mean 16:00 on a Western clock. [BBC News, via MyJoyOnline](https://www.myjoyonline.com/ethiopia-the-country-where-a-year-lasts-13-months/) The practical rule is to ask which clock a person means, and many Ethiopians switch easily between the two.

## The millennium of 2007

Because Ethiopia's count is behind, it reached the year 2000 while the West was in 2007. Voice of America reported that the country would celebrate its millennium on Wednesday, 12 September 2007, and attributed the gap to "disparities between the Ethiopian Orthodox Church and the Roman Catholic Church" over the date of creation. [VOA News, 2007](https://voanews.com/a/a-13-2007-09-10-voa6-66787127/565493.html) (Note that the more precise cause is the Incarnation, as described above; VOA's wording is a journalistic simplification.) Celebrations began with a cultural event in Addis Ababa on the night of 11 September and a New Year's Eve concert; Al Jazeera reported ticket prices of 270 to 330 US dollars against an average annual per capita income that peaked at about 160 dollars in 2005, which critics cited as proof the event was out of reach for most Ethiopians. [Al Jazeera, September 2007](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/africa/2007/09/20085251237751480.html) In the Ethiopian count, that day was Meskerem 1, 2000.

## Closing

The Ethiopian calendar is best understood as a different, equally old answer to a shared question, not as a delay. It has even months, a short year-end month, an era inherited from Alexandria, and a clock that begins at dawn. Visitors who learn to read all three find that the country's festivals, fasts and everyday appointments make more sense.
