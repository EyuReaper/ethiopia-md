# The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD): Energy, Finance and the Nile Dispute

The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (ታላቁ የኢትዮጵያ ህዳሴ ግድብ, GERD) stands on the Blue Nile, called the _Abay_ in Ethiopia, in the Benishangul-Gumuz Region about 14 kilometers from the Sudanese border. It was formally inaugurated on September 9, 2025, after construction began in 2011 ([Al Jazeera: Ethiopia inaugurates GERD dam amid downstream tensions with Egypt, Sudan](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/9/9/ethiopia-inaugurates-gerd-dam-amid-downstream-tensions-with-egypt-sudan)). With an installed capacity of 5,150 megawatts it is Africa's largest hydropower plant, and its reservoir can hold 74 billion cubic meters of water ([ASCE Civil Engineering: Giant Ethiopian dam promises greater flood control and water management](https://www.asce.org/publications-and-news/civil-engineering-source/civil-engineering-magazine/issues/magazine-issue/article/2025/07/giant-ethiopian-dam-promises-greater-flood-control-and-water-management)). For Ethiopia the dam is a national development project. Egypt and Sudan, which depend on the river downstream, oppose the way it has been filled and operated without a binding agreement. This article covers the engineering, the money, and each country's position.

## Engineering and Scale

The main dam is a roller-compacted concrete gravity structure. ASCE's July 2025 account, by Assefa M. Melesse and Alemayehu Gebriel, gives it a height of 145 meters and a length of 1,780 meters, with a 5-kilometer, 50-meter-high saddle dam closing a low point in the surrounding terrain. Full, the reservoir covers about 1,874 square kilometers at 640 meters above sea level. Thirteen turbines sit in two powerhouses ([ASCE](https://www.asce.org/publications-and-news/civil-engineering-source/civil-engineering-magazine/issues/magazine-issue/article/2025/07/giant-ethiopian-dam-promises-greater-flood-control-and-water-management)).

Figures vary with how they are measured. The main contractor, the Italian firm Webuild, describes the dam as 170 meters tall and 1,800 meters long at the crest, with 10.7 million cubic meters of concrete, a reservoir stretching 172 kilometers, an expected annual output of 15,700 gigawatt-hours, and more than 25,000 people, mostly Ethiopians, employed on the project ([Webuild: Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam inaugurated](https://www.webuildgroup.com/en/media/press-releases/grand-ethopian-renaissance-dam-gerd-inaugurated/)).

## Paying for the Dam

Al Jazeera and Mongabay both put the dam's cost at about 5 billion US dollars ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/9/9/ethiopia-inaugurates-gerd-dam-amid-downstream-tensions-with-egypt-sudan); [Mongabay: Ethiopia's Renaissance mega dam fuels energy hopes and regional anxiety](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/02/ethiopias-renaissance-mega-dam-fuels-energy-hopes-and-regional-anxiety/)). The project is widely described in Ethiopia as built without foreign loans for the dam itself. Public participation was central to that story: according to the GERD Public Participation Coordination Office, more than 23.6 billion birr had been raised from the public through bonds and gifts by September 2025 ([The Reporter: More Than a Dam](https://www.thereporterethiopia.com/46843/)). Addis Fortune reports a similar total of more than 20 billion birr over almost 15 years, nearly all of it from citizens' bonds and donations, including over one billion birr from diaspora bonds and gifts and smaller sums sent by text message ([Addis Fortune: Nationals Contribute 20B Br to GERD Project](https://addisfortune.news/?p=40160)).

These public contributions covered only part of the total. Mongabay describes the dam as financed mainly by Ethiopians and their government, along with Chinese loans and investment ([Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/02/ethiopias-renaissance-mega-dam-fuels-energy-hopes-and-regional-anxiety/)).

## Power at Home and for Export

Electricity access is the dam's domestic justification. Mongabay, citing the World Bank, reports that 56 percent of Ethiopians lacked basic electricity access, that more than 92 percent of households cook with biomass, and that the government's national energy compact targets 75 percent access by 2030 ([Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/02/ethiopias-renaissance-mega-dam-fuels-energy-hopes-and-regional-anxiety/)). Ethiopia already sells power to Kenya, Sudan and Djibouti; a 2022 agreement with Kenya starts at 200 megawatts and rises to 400 megawatts over 25 years, and Tanzania is expected to buy later. Ethiopian Electric Power says no power has been exported at the expense of domestic demand.

Mongabay could find no official environmental and social impact assessment online, and the Ministry of Water and Energy did not respond to its questions. Critics it quotes say the dam's environmental impact is "not well assessed" ([Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/02/ethiopias-renaissance-mega-dam-fuels-energy-hopes-and-regional-anxiety/)).

## Three Positions on the Nile

**Ethiopia.** Ethiopia's highlands supply about 80 percent of the water that flows downstream to Sudan and Egypt, yet Ethiopia was not a party to the 1929 Nile agreement or the 1959 Egypt–Sudan agreement, which allocated 55.5 billion cubic meters a year to Egypt and 18.5 billion to Sudan. Ethiopia argues that those treaties ignore its needs and treats the dam as a matter of sovereignty ([Carnegie Endowment: The Dam That Broke Open an Ethiopia-Egypt Dispute](https://carnegieendowment.org/middle-east/research/2021/04/the-dam-that-broke-open-an-ethiopia-egypt-dispute)). At the inauguration Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed called the dam "a shared opportunity" for the region ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/9/9/ethiopia-inaugurates-gerd-dam-amid-downstream-tensions-with-egypt-sudan)).

**Egypt.** Egypt depends on the Nile for more than 90 percent of its water ([IDOS: The Declaration of Principles on Ethiopia's Renaissance Dam](https://www.idos-research.de/die-aktuelle-kolumne/article/the-declaration-of-principles-on-ethiopias-renaissance-dam-a-breakthrough-or-another-unfair-deal/)), and its rainfall averages about 18 millimeters a year. Its central fear is the operation of the dam during multi-year droughts. Egypt sought a slow fill over 12 to 21 years and treats unilateral filling as a breach of the 2015 Declaration of Principles ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/middle-east/research/2021/04/the-dam-that-broke-open-an-ethiopia-egypt-dispute)). On the day of the inauguration, Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty wrote to the UN Security Council calling the dam "an existential threat" to the 150 million citizens of the downstream countries and reserving Egypt's right to take "all appropriate measures" ([Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/02/ethiopias-renaissance-mega-dam-fuels-energy-hopes-and-regional-anxiety/)).

**Sudan.** Sudan's position is mixed. It stands to gain cheaper electricity, easier irrigation and less flooding, but worries about the safety of its own Roseires Dam and about how much water will arrive without coordination ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/middle-east/research/2021/04/the-dam-that-broke-open-an-ethiopia-egypt-dispute)). At the inauguration, Roseires managers told Al Jazeera that the GERD had helped control overflow and reduced the silt and debris that had cut their reservoir's storage by a third. The week before, however, Sudan had joined Egypt in a statement calling Ethiopia's actions "unilateral" ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/9/9/ethiopia-inaugurates-gerd-dam-amid-downstream-tensions-with-egypt-sudan)). In July 2026, after falling Blue Nile levels put several Khartoum drinking-water stations out of service, a Sudanese water expert called for a legally binding operating agreement building on an October 2022 Sudan–Ethiopia information-sharing arrangement ([Radio Dabanga: Water expert urges binding GERD agreement after Nile levels fall in Sudan](https://www.dabangasudan.org/en?p=20135169)).

## Negotiations and the Research Record

The three countries signed a Declaration of Principles in Khartoum on March 23, 2015. Ethiopia committed to avoid significant harm to Egypt and Sudan and to mitigate any harm that occurred; the declaration made no reference to the older treaties or to Egypt's acquired share ([IDOS](https://www.idos-research.de/die-aktuelle-kolumne/article/the-declaration-of-principles-on-ethiopias-renaissance-dam-a-breakthrough-or-another-unfair-deal/)). Talks on filling and operating rules continued for years without a deal. On December 19, 2023, Egypt announced that the latest round had failed; Egypt's water ministry said "the path of negotiations has now ended" and that it would monitor the dam's filling and operation ([The National: Egypt says deadline over for Ethiopia dam negotiations after talks fail](https://thenationalnews.com/mena/2023/12/19/egypt-ethiopia-gerd-nile-dam)). Ethiopia's foreign ministry rejected "the misrepresentation of its position by Egypt," accused Egypt of a "colonial mindset," and repeated that it would not accept a legally binding operating agreement, arguing that non-binding recommendations should be enough ([The National: Ethiopia accuses Egypt of 'colonial mindset' as Nile dam talks fail](https://thenationalnews.com/mena/egypt/2023/12/20/ethiopia-gerd-egypt-talks)). In January 2026, US President Donald Trump offered to mediate ([Mongabay](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/02/ethiopias-renaissance-mega-dam-fuels-energy-hopes-and-regional-anxiety/)).

Independent modeling suggests the dispute is more about coordination than fixed shares. A 2020 study in _Nature Communications_ by Kevin Wheeler and colleagues found that the Aswan High Dam reservoir could fall during GERD filling to levels not seen in recent decades, though Egypt's risk of shortage stayed fairly low. After filling, the dam could benefit Ethiopia and Sudan without much harm to Egyptian water users, but a severe multi-year drought would require close coordination among the three countries ([Wheeler et al., Nature Communications 11, 5222 (2020): Understanding and managing new risks on the Nile with the GERD](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-19089-x)).

The GERD's power is meant to support an economy whose other flagship is the national carrier; see [Ethiopian Airlines](/en/economy/ethiopian-airlines-aviation-hub). The [Economy Hub](/en/economy) gives the wider context.
