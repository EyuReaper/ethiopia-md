# The Ethiopian Commodity Exchange: Marketing Crops Since 2008

## 30-second overview

The Ethiopian Commodity Exchange (ECX) is a spot market for agricultural goods, started in 2008 to make trade in coffee and other crops more transparent. According to the PBS documentary series Wide Angle, its founder Eleni Gabre-Madhin rang the opening bell on 24 April 2008 ([PBS Wide Angle: The Market Maker](https://www.pbs.org/wnet/wideangle/?p=5184)). Its design combined graded and certified commodities, stored in exchange warehouses, with open-outcry trading and quick price dissemination. For several years coffee sold by private washing stations had to pass through it; from 2017 that rule was relaxed. This article explains how it worked, what research found, and what its own leadership says about its current position. We treat the evidence on its welfare effects as mixed.

## What It Was Designed to Do

Wikipedia, citing the exchange's founding proclamation, says the ECX was established in April 2008 as a private company owned jointly by market actors, exchange members and the Ethiopian government, and was led by Eleni Gabre-Madhin, a former economist at the International Food Policy Research Institute ([Wikipedia: Ethiopia Commodity Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia_Commodity_Exchange)). As of November 2010 spot contracts covered coffee, sesame, navy beans, maize and wheat, and by 2011 coffee and oilseeds and pulses held nearly equal shares of volume; there were 55 warehouses in 17 locations in July 2011, according to the same page, which has not been updated with later figures.

## How Trading Worked

PBS describes an open-outcry system: buyers and sellers call out price, quantity and grade in public, private discussions are not allowed, and a deal is sealed with a high-five before the paperwork at a requisition desk. A computer checks that the seller's inventory is in an exchange warehouse and the buyer's money is in an exchange account, and within four seconds of approval the price shows on screens in 16 markets nationwide. The seller is paid the next day, and the buyer can collect the goods at any warehouse holding similar stock, so a farmer in one town can sell to a buyer elsewhere without moving the goods ([PBS Wide Angle](https://www.pbs.org/wnet/wideangle/?p=5184)). This description reflects the early years and the open-outcry floor, and we have not verified how trading is conducted today.

## What Research Found

One study, a 2015 GlobalFood discussion paper by Camilla Andersson, Mintewab Bezabih and Andrea Mannberg, examined retail price data from 2007 to 2012 and used the staggered opening of connected warehouses to test whether the exchange reduced price gaps between markets. As summarized in search results, since the full text was blocked when we tried to open it, it found that the average price spread between pairs of markets fell by about 0.86 to 1.775 birr when both had an operating warehouse. The authors link this to warehouse receipts, which serve as proof of ownership of stored goods and can support bank loans ([Andersson, Bezabih and Mannberg: Price discovery in the Ethiopian Commodity Exchange, GlobalFood DP 72](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/209963/files/GlobalFood_DP72.pdf)). We cite this as a result of one study on retail prices, not as a verdict on farmers' incomes.

## The Coffee Mandate and Its Reform

An IIED report from 2021 says trading coffee through the ECX was required for most market participants from 2008, with cooperatives and large plantations exempt. It says reforms in 2017, which came after pressure from exporters, foreign importers and donors, allowed coffee trading outside the exchange again, and it notes that the author examines whether smallholders and cooperatives could take part in the reforms ([IIED: Ethiopian coffee marketing reforms and smallholder coffee producers](https://www.iied.org/node/73331)). The report's abstract is all we could read, so we cannot report its findings on prices or incomes.

Daily Coffee News reported in April 2017, based on an announcement from the trading firm Trabocca, that under a trial starting on 30 April 2017 exporters with a valid license could sell directly to international buyers; trucks of parchment coffee that were not sold within three days of arriving at warehouses in the capital would go to the exchange, with traceability maintained. The article stressed that this came from a company's release and that official confirmation was not shown ([Daily Coffee News: Ethiopia to loosen ECX restrictions](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2017/04/11/ethiopia-to-loosen-ecx-restrictions-says-green-trader-trabocca)).

## The Exchange Today

An Addis Fortune article, which carries no visible publication date, reports that the exchange's chief executive, Mergia Bayissa, told Parliament and audit officials that traders "were once required to use the ECX but now have other options," that export coffee is sold at prices set weekly by the coffee authority, and that "these prices are not attractive to sellers, exporters, or suppliers." He said the exchange had been "forced to see coffee at prices lower than the actual market value," which pushed customers away. The article also says the exchange trades 28 commodities with 510 members and now works as a spot market with a price floor, and it cites falling revenue (down 22 percent over three years), a shortfall against planned volume and a 29 percent fall in coffee exports through the exchange ([Addis Fortune: Commodity Exchange Buckles Under Coffee Price Controls Weight](https://addisfortune.news/?p=45675)). These are the chief executive's statements, as reported by one newspaper, and the coffee authority's view is not included. Because the page is undated, we do not give a year for the figures.

## Points of Disagreement

- The 2015 study found narrower price spreads where connected warehouses operated, and PBS presents the exchange as a transparency tool.
- IIED says the 2017 reform followed pressure from exporters, foreign importers and donors; it does not, in the part we could read, set out their arguments.
- Today the exchange's own leadership, as reported by Addis Fortune, blames administrative coffee pricing for its decline.

These positions are not mutually exclusive, and we have not found a recent independent evaluation of the exchange's overall effect on farmers.

## Closing

The ECX tried to give a fragmented agricultural market a transparent, warehouse-based price system, and its role has shrunk where the state set prices or allowed direct sales. A stronger article would add an independent post-2017 evaluation and the exchange's own recent annual reports.
